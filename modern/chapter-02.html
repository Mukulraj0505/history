European entry into India was initially driven primarily by trade, especially the lucrative trade in spices and other Asian commodities.

Core Transformation:

The European presence in India changed gradually from commercial activity to military and political intervention.

However, this transformation did not happen simultaneously for all European powers.

2. Why Did Europeans Search for a Sea Route to India?

European Demand for Indian and Asian Goods

  • Spices (Pepper, Cinnamon, Cloves, Cardamom)
  • Cotton textiles (Calico, Muslin, Chintz)
  • Silk and Saltpetre (used for gunpowder)
  • Indigo, Precious stones and luxury goods

Major Reasons

  1. Fall of Constantinople (1453): Captured by Ottoman Turks, who blocked the overland trade route and levied exorbitant duties.
  2. Monopoly of Italian Republics: Venice and Genoa controlled Mediterranean trade routes, shutting out Western European nations (Portugal, Spain).
  3. Renaissance Spirit & Navigation: Advances in shipbuilding, astrolabes, mariner's compass, and cartography (spurred by Prince Henry the Navigator of Portugal).
  4. Papal Support: Pope Alexander VI issued the Bull of 1493, followed by the Treaty of Tordesillas (1494) dividing the non-Christian world between Portugal and Spain.
UPSC Trap:

The search for a sea route was not because Europeans had suddenly "discovered" India. India had thriving, millennia-old commercial links with West Asia, Rome, Africa, and Southeast Asia. What was discovered was merely a direct maritime route via Cape of Good Hope bypassing Ottoman/Arab intermediaries.

3. India Before the Arrival of the Portuguese

India was already deeply integrated into the Indian Ocean trading system.

  • Arab merchants were dominant intermediaries in the Arabian Sea.
  • Indian merchant communities (Gujaratis, Chettis, Marakkars) actively participated in maritime trade.
  • Ports like Calicut, Cochin, Cambay, Surat, and Masulipatnam were thriving cosmopolitan trade nodes.
  • Trade was generally peaceful and unarmed prior to Portuguese militarization.
Conceptual Point:

Europeans entered an already sophisticated commercial world; they did not create Indian Ocean trade. However, the Portuguese introduced armed maritime trade for the first time.

5. Vasco da Gama and the Discovery of the Sea Route

  • Vasco da Gama reached Calicut in May 1498 guided by a Gujarati pilot named Abdul Majid.
  • He was received by the Hindu ruler of Calicut, bearing the hereditary title Zamorin (Samuthiri).
  • The cargo of spices he brought back to Lisbon was sold for 60 times the cost of the entire voyage.
  • Vasco da Gama made a second voyage to India in 1502, establishing a factory at Cannanore and demanding the expulsion of all Muslim traders.
Most Important Chronology:

1488 → Bartolomeu Dias → Cape of Good Hope

1498 → Vasco da Gama → Calicut (First European to arrive by sea)

UPSC Trap:

Bartolomeu Dias did NOT reach India. He only reached the Cape of Good Hope (which he named Cape of Storms). Vasco da Gama completed the voyage to India.

6. Portuguese Strategy in India

The Portuguese established their maritime empire called the Estado da Índia (State of India).

Major Features

  • Choke-point Strategy: Secured key maritime chokepoints: Hormuz (Persian Gulf), Malacca (Straits of Malacca), Aden, and Goa.
  • Cartaz System: A naval licensing/pass system where every merchant ship in the Indian Ocean had to purchase a pass from the Portuguese or face confiscation and sinking.
  • Feitorias (Fortified Factories): Armed fortified trading posts along strategic coasts.
  • Feitorias & Armadas: Armed naval squadrons patrolling trade routes to enforce monopolies.
Cartaz System & UPSC Relevance:

Even the Mughal Emperor Akbar had to take Cartaz passes from the Portuguese for his royal Haj ships sailing from Surat to Mecca.

7. Francisco de Almeida (1505–1509)

Appointed the first Viceroy of Portuguese India by King Manuel I.

Blue Water Policy (Cartaz / Mare Clausum)

Almeida believed Portugal should remain master of the seas rather than build a territorial land empire in India. His policy was to destroy Muslim maritime commerce and establish undisputed Portuguese naval hegemony across the Indian Ocean.

Remember:

Francisco de Almeida → Blue Water Policy (Naval dominance over territorial land conquest).

8. Battle of Diu — 1509

The Battle of Diu (1509) decisively established European naval dominance in Asian waters for the next 400 years.

Belligerents

  • Portuguese: Commanded by Francisco de Almeida.
  • Combined Muslim Coalition: Sultanate of Gujarat (Mahmud Begada), Mamluk Sultanate of Egypt, and Zamorin of Calicut, backed by the Ottoman Empire and Venice.
Historical Significance:
  • Avenged the death of Almeida's son Lourenço at the Battle of Chaul (1508).
  • Shattered the combined naval power of Egypt and Gujarat.
  • Established absolute Portuguese maritime control over the Arabian Sea trade routes.

9. Afonso de Albuquerque (1509–1515)

Considered the real founder of Portuguese power in the East.

Major Contributions

  • Capture of Goa (1510): Captured Goa from Ismail Adil Shah, Sultan of Bijapur. Goa became the first Indian territory under European dominion since Alexander.
  • Strategic Chokepoints: Captured Malacca (1511) and Ormuz (1515) to bottleneck Asian maritime trade.
  • Abolition of Sati: Abolished the practice of Sati within Portuguese-controlled Goa (first European administrator to ban Sati).
  • Policy of Settlement & Intermarriage: Encouraged Portuguese soldiers to marry local Indian women (forming the Anglo-Portuguese/Luso-Indian population).
  • Introduced new varieties of fruits and recruited native Indian sepoys.
High-Yield Association:

Afonso de Albuquerque → Real Founder → Capture of Goa (1510) → Abolished Sati in Goa → Intermarriage policy.

9A. Nino da Cunha (1529–1538) — Key UPSC Additions

  • Shift of Capital (1530): Officially transferred the Portuguese headquarters from Cochin to Goa.
  • Expansion in Gujarat: Secured Bassein (1534) and Diu from Bahadur Shah of Gujarat during Bahadur Shah's conflict with Mughal Emperor Humayun.
  • Death of Bahadur Shah (1537): Bahadur Shah was invited aboard a Portuguese ship for negotiations off Diu, where a scuffle broke out and he drowned.
  • Expansion to Bengal: Established settlements at Hooghly and Chittagong.

11. Portuguese Religious Policy

Portuguese expansion was closely tied to Catholic proselytization (Padroado system).

  • Arrival of the Jesuit order; St. Francis Xavier arrived in Goa in 1542.
  • Destruction of Hindu temples and forced conversions.
  • Goa Inquisition (1560): Established to punish newly converted Christians suspected of practicing their former religions secretly.
UPSC Trap:

The Goa Inquisition was established in 1560, decades after Albuquerque's death. It led to severe religious intolerance and persecution of non-Catholics.

12. Portuguese Relations with the Mughals

Initially, Akbar invited Jesuit missions (Father Monserrate and Father Aquaviva) to his court at Fatehpur Sikri. However, relations deteriorated over piracy and slave raiding.

The Siege of Hooghly (1632)

Under Shah Jahan, Mughal forces commanded by Bengal Governor Qasim Khan besieged and captured Hooghly. The Portuguese were punished for illicit trade, fortifying without permission, and abducting Mughal subjects into slavery.

Why Important?

It proved that Portuguese naval might could not shield their coastal factories when confronted by a determined continental land empire like the Mughals.

13. Decline of Portuguese Power in India

Cause Historical Explanation
Religious Intolerance Alienated local Hindu and Muslim rulers and populations through aggressive conversions and the Inquisition.
Discovery of Brazil Diverted Portugal's colonial resources, manpower, and commercial focus toward South America.
Union with Spain (1580–1640) Dragged Portugal into Spain's European wars, making Portuguese fleets targets for the English and Dutch.
Rise of Powerful Indian Neighbours Marathas captured Salsette and Bassein (Chimaji Appa, 1739); Mughals seized Hooghly (1632).
Rivalry with Dutch and English Superior naval architecture, better joint-stock financial models, and greater naval discipline displaced Portuguese maritime supremacy.

14. Significance of the Portuguese in India

  • Opened the direct maritime route connecting India to Europe.
  • Introduced European naval cannon-broadside combat and armed convoy tactics.
  • Columbian Exchange in India: Introduced tobacco, potato, tomato, chillies, cashew, pineapple, groundnut, maize, papaya, and guavas.
  • Printing Press: Established the first printing press in India at Goa in 1556.
  • Scientific Studies: Published Garcia da Orta's treatise on Indian medicinal plants (1563).
  • Introduced Manueline and Baroque architecture and silversmith craft to the Konkan coast.
Prelims High-Yield Fact:

Tobacco, Chilli, Potato, Cashew nut, and Pineapple were all brought to India by the Portuguese from the Americas.

15. The Dutch in India

In 1602, the States General of the Netherlands amalgamated several trading companies into the Vereenigde Oostindische Compagnie (VOC).

VOC (Dutch East India Company):

Chartered in 1602 with powers to wage war, conclude treaties, acquire territories, and build fortresses.

Core Strategy

The primary interest of the Dutch was the Spice Islands (Indonesia / Moluccas). Their Indian factories served primarily to procure textiles to trade for spices in the Indonesian archipelago.

18. Decline of the Dutch in India

  • Battle of Colachel (1741): King Marthanda Varma of Travancore crushed the Dutch VOC fleet. This ended Dutch trade monopoly in Kerala and remains the first instance of an Asian power decisively defeating a European naval force in India.
  • Amboyna Massacre (1623): In Indonesia, the Dutch massacred English merchants. A mutual compromise followed: the British left the Indonesian spice islands to the Dutch, while the Dutch ceded commercial dominance of India to the British.
  • Battle of Bedara / Chinsurah (1759): The English under Robert Clive routed the Dutch army and navy in Bengal, eliminating the Dutch as a political player in India.
  • Dutch priority remained Indonesia (Sumatra, Java, Spice Islands) where profit margins on cloves and nutmeg were vastly higher.
UPSC Battles Trap:

Colachel (1741): Travancore (Marthanda Varma) vs Dutch (Dutch defeated).
Bedara (1759): English vs Dutch (Dutch definitively defeated in India).

19. The English East India Company

Formed in 1599 by a merchant group called the "Merchant Adventurers" and chartered by Queen Elizabeth I on 31 December 1600 under the formal name: "Governor and Company of Merchants of London trading into the East Indies."

High-Yield Fact:

Royal Charter granted a 15-year monopoly on English trade east of the Cape of Good Hope.

20. English Entry: Hawkins & Sir Thomas Roe

1. Captain William Hawkins (1608–1611)

  • Arrived at Surat in August 1608 in the flagship Hector.
  • Travelled to Agra to visit the Mughal court of Jahangir (April 1609).
  • Spoke fluent Turki; Jahangir granted him a Mansab of 400 with a salary and the title "English Khan".
  • Prevented from establishing a factory at Surat due to Portuguese intrigue at court.

2. Sir Thomas Roe (1615–1619)

  • Sent as official ambassador of King James I to Emperor Jahangir's court.
  • Secured imperial farmans to set up English factories at Surat, Agra, Ahmedabad, and Broach.
UPSC Chronology Trap:

Hawkins arrived in 1608/09 (Jahangir's court); Thomas Roe arrived later in 1615.

22. Surat & The Battle of Swally (1612)

In 1612, an English naval fleet under Captain Thomas Best defeated a superior Portuguese squadron at Swally Hole near Surat.

Significance of Battle of Swally (1612):

Impressed by British naval prowess, Mughal Emperor Jahangir issued a farman in 1613 permitting the English to erect their first permanent factory at Surat under Thomas Aldworth.

23. Bombay & Gerald Aungier

Bombay was ceded to King Charles II of England in 1661 as part of the royal dowry upon his marriage to Portuguese princess Catherine of Braganza. In 1668, the Crown leased Bombay to the East India Company for an annual rent of £10.

Gerald Aungier (1669–1677):

Known as the true founder of Bombay. He fortified the island, set up a mint, established courts of justice, built docks, and offered complete religious freedom, attracting Gujarati traders, Parsis, and Armenian merchants.

Trap:

Bombay was acquired through royal diplomatic marriage/dowry and leased to the Company, NOT through conquest.

24. Madras, Golden Farman & Fort St. George

  • Golden Farman of Golconda (1632): The Sultan of Golconda granted the English the right to trade freely in his kingdom's ports for a fixed payment of 500 pagodas annually.
  • Acquisition of Madras (1639): Francis Day obtained the grant of Madraspatnam from the Raja of Chandragiri (Damerla Venkatadri).
  • Fort St. George (1640): Erected as a fortified trading post; became the first English fortress in India.

25. Calcutta & Farrukhsiyar's Farman (1717)

Foundation of Calcutta

In 1690, Job Charnock established a factory at Sutanuti. In 1698, the Company purchased the zamindari rights of three villages: Sutanuti, Kalikata, and Gobindapur for ₹1,200 from the Sabarna Roy Choudhury family. In 1700, the factory was fortified and named Fort William (with Sir Charles Eyre as first President).

Farrukhsiyar's Farman (1717) — "Magna Carta of the Company"

Negotiated by the John Surman Embassy (Dr. William Hamilton cured Emperor Farrukhsiyar of a painful disease). Farrukhsiyar issued three imperial farmans:

Region Privilege Granted
Bengal Exemption from all custom duties for a nominal annual payment of ₹3,000; right to issue Dastaks (trade permits); right to rent 38 additional villages around Calcutta.
Surat Exemption from all duties for a fixed annual payment of ₹10,000.
Bombay Coins minted by Company at Bombay made legal tender across the entire Mughal Empire.
UPSC Trap:

Misuse of Dastaks by Company private servants (intended only for Company goods) became the primary bone of contention leading to the confrontation with Siraj-ud-Daulah and the Battle of Plassey (1757).

26. The French in India

The French were the last of the major European powers to arrive in India. The Compagnie des Indes Orientales was founded in 1664 by Finance Minister Jean-Baptiste Colbert during the reign of King Louis XIV.

Structural Difference:

Unlike the private joint-stock English and Dutch companies, the French Company was completely state-controlled, financed, and directed by the French Monarchy.

28. Joseph François Dupleix (1742–1754)

Appointed Director-General of French possessions in India in 1742. A brilliant diplomat and strategist who conceived the idea of building an Indian territorial empire.

Dupleix's Master Strategy

  • Intervened in domestic succession disputes among Indian princes.
  • Used European-trained Indian infantry (sepoys) led by European officers to defeat massive Indian cavalry armies.
  • Offered military protection to local rulers in exchange for territorial revenues and trade monopolies (precursor to Wellesley's Subsidiary Alliance).
  • Tragic flaw: Lacked naval support, had hostile relations with French military commanders (La Bourdonnais), and was recalled prematurely by the French government in 1754.

30. First Carnatic War (1746–1748)

Background & Causes

Direct echo of the War of the Austrian Succession (1740–1748) in Europe.

Major Events

  • English naval squadron under Barnet captured French merchant ships.
  • Dupleix summoned Admiral La Bourdonnais from Mauritius, who attacked and captured Madras (1746).
  • Battle of St. Thomé (Battle of Adyar, 1746): Nawab of Carnatic, Anwar-ud-din, sent 10,000 troops under Mahfuz Khan to reclaim Madras. A tiny French force of 230 Europeans and 700 Indian sepoys under Captain Paradise routed the Nawab's massive army.
Significance of Battle of St. Thomé:

Revealed the enormous superiority of small, disciplined European-trained infantry armed with modern flintlock muskets and rapid-firing artillery over massive, indisciplined Indian armies.

Conclusion

Ended by the Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748). Madras was restored to the English in exchange for the French receiving Louisbourg in North America.

31. Second Carnatic War (1749–1754)

Causes: Succession Disputes

Throne French Supported Candidate English Supported Candidate
Hyderabad (Nizam) Muzaffar Jang (Grandson) Nasir Jang (Son)
Carnatic (Nawab) Chanda Sahib Muhammad Ali (Son of Anwar-ud-din)

Key Events

  • Battle of Ambur (1749): French, Chanda Sahib, and Muzaffar Jang defeated and killed Anwar-ud-din. Chanda Sahib became Nawab of Carnatic.
  • Muzaffar Jang became Nizam of Hyderabad and rewarded Dupleix with territories and revenues; French general Bussy stationed at Hyderabad.
  • Siege of Arcot (1751): Robert Clive (then a young clerk-turned-officer) executed a diversionary strike and captured Arcot (capital of Carnatic) with just 210 men, heroically defending it for 53 days.
  • This broke the siege of Trichinopoly, saved Muhammad Ali, and shattered French military prestige.

Conclusion

French Directors, exhausted by financial losses, recalled Dupleix in 1754 and sent Godeheu, who signed the Treaty of Pondicherry (1754). Both parties agreed not to interfere in the internal affairs of Indian princes.

32. Third Carnatic War (1756–1763)

Background & Causes

Triggered by the outbreak of the Seven Years' War (1756–1763) in Europe.

Key Events

  • In 1757, the British captured the French factory of Chandernagore in Bengal (following Battle of Plassey).
  • French government sent Count de Lally to India. Lally made the strategic blunder of recalling Bussy from Hyderabad, ending French influence at the Nizam's court.
  • Battle of Wandiwash (22 January 1760): British forces under Sir Eyre Coote decisively smashed the French army under Count de Lally. Bussy was taken prisoner.
  • British captured Pondicherry in 1761, demolishing its fortifications.

Conclusion

Ended by the Treaty of Paris (1763). French factories (Pondicherry, Chandernagore, Mahe) were returned, but the French were prohibited from fortifying them or keeping troops. French political ambitions in India were extinguished forever.

34. English vs French — Why Did the English Win?

Dimension English Advantage French Limitation
Ownership & Structure Private Joint-Stock Company; flexible, commercial decisions taken swiftly without bureaucratic red tape. State-controlled Department; dependent on King's whims and slow monarchical funding.
Naval Superiority Royal Navy was the unchallenged master of the seas; could resupply bases continuously. French navy inferior and committed to European naval theatres.
Commercial Strength Massive commercial volume financed military expeditions; never neglected trade for conquest. Dupleix subordinated commerce to politics, bankrupting the company.
Strategic Base in Bengal Conquest of Bengal (Plassey, 1757) gave England endless financial and food resources for south Indian wars. Pondicherry constantly starved of funds and food supplies.
Distribution of Bases Three independent, fortified strategic hubs: Calcutta, Bombay, Madras. Only Pondicherry; if Pondicherry was isolated, French presence crumbled.
Military Leadership String of capable leaders: Clive, Lawrence, Eyre Coote. Only Dupleix and Bussy; Count de Lally was rash and alienated his subordinates.

36. European Powers — Sequence of Arrival vs Company Charter

Classic UPSC Chronology Trap:

Do not confuse the order of Company Formation with the order of Arrival in India!

Sequence Order of Formation of Company Order of Arrival / First Factory in India
1st Portuguese Estado da Índia (1498) Portuguese (1498 – Calicut / 1503 – Cochin)

4th Danish East India Company (1616) Danish (1620 – Tranquebar)

Remember the Trap:

• Company Formation: Portuguese (1498) → English (1600) → Dutch (1602) → Danish (1616) → French (1664).
• Arrival in India: Portuguese (1498) → Dutch (1605) → English (1608) → Danish (1620) → French (1668).
English Company was founded BEFORE the Dutch, but the Dutch arrived in India BEFORE the English!

38. Factory ≠ Manufacturing Factory

In colonial terminology, a "factory" was an armed warehouse, commercial office, and residential quarters for the Company's commercial agents called "factors". No manufacturing took place there; goods purchased from local weavers and artisans were sorted, inspected, packed, and stored for loading onto Europe-bound ships.

39. Trading Company vs Sovereign State

European expansion was pioneered by chartered corporate entities rather than direct state armies.

Capital Source Private shareholders seeking dividends Royal treasury and monarchical revenues
Accountability Court of Directors / Board of Governors Monarch / Ministers (Colbert)
Incentive Cost-efficiency, profitability, return on equity Geopolitical glory, dynastic prestige, religious spread

41. NCERT Cross-Check — "From Trade to Territory"

  • Weakening Centre: Death of Aurangzeb (1707) triggered fragmentation into regional kingdoms (Bengal, Awadh, Hyderabad, Marathas).
  • Commercial Abuse: Farrukhsiyar's farman allowed duty-free trade for Company; company officials claimed exemption for personal private trade, causing massive revenue losses to Bengal Nawabs.
  • Black Hole Tragedy & Plassey: Fortification disputes at Calcutta led to Siraj-ud-Daulah capturing Fort William, culminating in Clive's conspiracy and the Battle of Plassey (1757).

42. Top 15 UPSC Prelims Traps for Quick Revision

1. Dias vs Vasco da Gama: Dias only rounded Cape of Good Hope (1488). Vasco da Gama was the first to reach India (Calicut, 1498).
2. Almeida vs Albuquerque: Almeida = Blue Water Policy & Battle of Diu (1509). Albuquerque = Capture of Goa (1510) & Sati ban.
3. Capital Shift: Portuguese capital was NOT initially Goa; it was Cochin until Nino da Cunha shifted it to Goa in 1530.
4. First Factory of British: First in South India was Masulipatnam (1611); first permanent factory in West India was Surat (1613).
5. Hawkins vs Roe: William Hawkins arrived in 1608/09; Sir Thomas Roe arrived later in 1615.
6. Farrukhsiyar's Farman: 1717 (Surman Embassy), not 1617. Coined the "Magna Carta" by Orme.
7. First French Factory: Established at Surat (1667) by François Caron, NOT Pondicherry.
8. First Carnatic War Treaty: Treaty of Aix-la-Chapelle (1748), NOT Treaty of Pondicherry.
9. Second Carnatic War Treaty: Treaty of Pondicherry (1754), which recalled Dupleix.
10. Third Carnatic War Treaty: Treaty of Paris (1763) following Battle of Wandiwash (1760).
11. Battle of Colachel (1741): Marthanda Varma of Travancore defeated the Dutch VOC, NOT the British.
12. Battle of Bedara (1759): British defeated the Dutch, ending Dutch political power in Bengal.
13. Swally (1612) vs Diu (1509): Diu = Portuguese defeated Egypt/Gujarat. Swally = English defeated Portuguese.
14. Dastak Privilege: Meant ONLY for Company goods; private trade by company servants was illegal.
15. Order of Arrival: Portuguese (1498) → Dutch (1605) → English (1608) → Danish (1620) → French (1668).

45. GS-I Mains Themes & Model Framework

Core Analytical Question

"Explain the factors that led to the transformation of the English East India Company from a commercial trading venture into a sovereign territorial power in 18th-century India."

1. Introduction

Established by the Royal Charter of 1600 as a mercantile joint-stock corporation, the EIC gradually transformed into a paramount political entity due to structural institutional advantages and the disintegration of the Mughal Empire.

2. European Structural Advantages

• Naval Dominance: Command of the sea lanes ensuring uninterrupted supply of troops, bullion, and munitions.
• Financial Resilience: Supported by the Bank of England and British national debt market, providing capital that private Indian or French entities could not match.
• Corporate Independence: Directors made autonomous decisions free from monarchical whims, unlike the state-run French Company.

3. Indian Domestic Context

• Post-1707 Mughal decline created regional power vacuums and succession civil wars (Carnatic, Hyderabad, Bengal).
• Indian rulers relied on European drill, artillery, and mercenaries, inviting European arbitration.

4. Military & Strategic Evolution

• Sepoy Army: Raising disciplined native infantry trained in European flintlock volley firing.
• Strategic Triumphs: Victory at Wandiwash (1760) eliminated European rivals; Plassey (1757) and Buxar (1764) unlocked the fertile fiscal revenues of Bengal.

5. Conclusion

As G.B. Malleson observed, British conquest of India was not accidental; it resulted from institutional superiority, mastery of the seas, and strategic exploitation of India's political fragmentation.
