CHAPTER 6

Chalcolithic Cultures: The Stone-Copper Phase

Regional Agro-Pastoral Traditions, Copper-Stone Metallurgy, Painted Ceramics (Ahar, Kayatha, Malwa, Jorwe), Settlement Layouts, Burial Rites, Inamgaon & Daimabad

UPSC Relevance & Analytical Framework

UPSC Core Perspective:

The Chalcolithic Age (Copper-Stone phase) represents the emergence of India's earliest metal-using village communities. In the Indian archaeological landscape, these cultures were non-urban, non-Harappan regional agro-pastoral societies that developed alongside, contemporary with, or in the wake of the mature Harappan world.

UPSC repeatedly tests Chalcolithic cultures across six interrelated axes: regional culture → representative archaeological site → river basin/geography → distinctive painted ceramic ware → burial/religious rituals → technological limitations (lack of bronze, high infant mortality, non-urban character).

High-Yield Prelims Focus — Key Facts & Trap Zone

1. Common Prelims Trap Zone — Scrutinize Absolute Statements

Trap 1: "The introduction of copper completely replaced the use of stone tools."

❌ Incorrect: The Chalcolithic represents a mixed, composite technological system (Copper + Stone). Pure copper was scarce, expensive, and too soft; everyday harvesting, cutting, and scraping continued to rely heavily on stone blades and microliths.

Trap 2: "Bronze was completely absent from all Chalcolithic contexts."

❌ Incorrect & Too Absolute: While non-Harappan Chalcolithic cultures predominantly used unalloyed copper alongside continuing stone technology and lacked systematic bronze alloying for utilitarian tools, the blanket claim that "bronze was completely absent" is too absolute. Low-tin bronze objects and copper-tin alloys have been documented in some regional prehistoric sequences, particularly towards later phases (most spectacularly demonstrated by the 60 kg Daimabad bronze hoard). The safe UPSC formulation is: "The Chalcolithic is characterised by the increasing use of copper alongside continuing stone technology; metal use varied by region and phase."

Trap 3: "All Chalcolithic cultures developed chronologically after the decline of the Harappan Civilisation."

❌ Incorrect: Chalcolithic cultures were chronologically diverse: Kayatha (c. 2450–2000 BCE) and Ganeshwar were contemporary with the Mature Harappan phase, while Jorwe (c. 1400–700 BCE) flourished long after Harappan urban decline.

Trap 4: "Chalcolithic communities were the first in India to use painted pottery."

❌ Incorrect: Painted pottery already existed in pre-Harappan and Neolithic horizons (e.g. Mehrgarh II/III, Amri, Kot Diji). The correct formulation is: painted pottery became an indispensable diagnostic hallmark of Chalcolithic cultures, but they were NOT the first to invent it.

Trap 5: "Chalcolithic societies created the earliest settled village communities in India."

❌ Incorrect: Established village farming life was pioneered during the Neolithic Period (e.g., Mehrgarh in Balochistan c. 7000 BCE, Lahuradewa in Uttar Pradesh). Chalcolithic cultures developed upon, expanded, and regionally diversified existing village traditions.

Trap 6: "Non-Harappan Chalcolithic people were universally unacquainted with the use of burnt bricks."

❌ Incorrect: Gilund in the Banas basin, Rajasthan, preserves clear archaeological evidence of kiln-burnt brick structures. The safe UPSC formulation is: burnt-brick urban architecture was not a defining feature of most non-Harappan Chalcolithic village settlements (which predominantly used mud, mud-brick, reeds, and wattle-and-daub), but it is incorrect to state categorically that they did not know burnt bricks at all.

Trap 7: "Inamgaon in Maharashtra was an urban trading city of the Harappan Empire." (UPSC 2024 PYQ Link)

❌ Incorrect: Inamgaon is a major rural Chalcolithic settlement of the Jorwe culture located on the Ghod river in Maharashtra, famous for mud houses, public granaries, hydraulic weir, and burial rituals—NOT a Harappan city.

Trap 8: "Ahar culture in Rajasthan relied extensively on stone microlithic blade tools."

❌ Incorrect: Ahar is uniquely notable for the virtual absence of stone microliths. Because copper was readily available from nearby Aravalli ores, the people of Ahar manufactured axes, blades, and bangles directly from smelted copper.

Trap 9: "Ganeshwar-Jodhpura is simply another name for the Ahar-Banas culture of Rajasthan."

❌ Incorrect: Ganeshwar-Jodhpura was an autonomous copper-producing complex in north-east Rajasthan near the Khetri belt with heavy microlithic tools and incised orange pottery, acting as a metal feeder to Harappan Kalibangan, distinct from Ahar-Banas in Mewar.

Trap 10: "All Chalcolithic cultures shared a single uniform pottery tradition."

❌ Incorrect: Chalcolithic cultures are classified primarily by their distinct regional painted pottery: Ahar (Black-and-Red with white paintings), Kayatha (chocolate-slipped), Malwa (buff-slipped with animal motifs), and Jorwe (matte black-on-red with spouted vases).

Trap 11: "Daimabad in Maharashtra belongs exclusively to the early Neolithic phase."

❌ Incorrect: Daimabad is the largest Chalcolithic site in Maharashtra (Jorwe culture), famous for its Late Harappan phase and the extraordinary discovery of four solid-cast bronze sculptures (charioteer, rhino, elephant, buffalo).

Trap 12: "Navdatoli on the Narmada River is a key site of the Jorwe culture."

❌ Incorrect: Navdatoli belongs to the Malwa culture of Madhya Pradesh (excavated by H.D. Sankalia opposite Maheshwar), famous for yielding the richest variety of cultivated food grains in prehistoric India.

Trap 13: "Ochre Coloured Pottery (OCP) and Copper Hoards are identical to the Jorwe and Malwa cultures."

❌ Incorrect: OCP and Copper Hoards represent an entirely distinct archaeological complex localized in the Upper Ganga-Yamuna Doab, characterized by unique weaponry (anthropomorphic figures, antennae swords, harpoons).

Trap 14: "Ganeshwar was a consumer city that imported finished copper items from Kalibangan."

❌ Incorrect: The relationship was the exact reverse: Ganeshwar was an indigenous copper-producing center near the Khetri mines that supplied copper ingots and tools to the Harappans at Kalibangan.

Trap 15: "Burial orientation was strictly uniform: north-to-south in Maharashtra and east-to-west in South India."

❌ Incorrect: There is no single universal rule regarding burial orientation for all Maharashtra or South Indian Chalcolithic communities. Urn burials (particularly of children) and extended pit burials varied considerably in orientation, grave goods, and ritual practices across sites and cultural phases.

Trap 16: "Adult burials at Inamgaon were made with the feet pointing North and heads pointing South."

❌ Incorrect: Adults were buried inside house floors in an extended position with the head pointing North and feet pointing South; furthermore, the feet were intentionally chopped off at the ankles (anti-ghost ritual).

Trap 17: "Chalcolithic societies were completely egalitarian with no evidence of hierarchy or fortification."

❌ Incorrect: Excavations reveal clear settlement hierarchy and defensive planning: Kayatha, Eran, Balathal, and Inamgaon preserve mud ramparts, walls, or moats. Inamgaon has a 5-room chief's house contrasting with modest single-room huts.

Trap 18: "All female figurines and bull depictions prove institutionalized Mother Goddess and deity worship."

❌ Incorrect: Female terracotta figurines and bull representations occur across several Chalcolithic sites, but these finds should not be used as automatic proof that all communities worshipped an institutionalized "mother goddess" or a single bull deity. Their religious significance remains an archaeological interpretation, not an established theological dogma.

Trap 19: "Eastern Chalcolithic communities lived solely on fish."

❌ Incorrect: While fishing supplemented diet at riverine settlements (e.g. Chirand, Pandu Rajar Dhibi), eastern Chalcolithic communities practiced active rice agriculture, animal husbandry, and crafts.

Trap 20: "Prabhas was simply the ancient name of the town of Somnath."

❌ Incorrect: Prabhas is the broader ancient sacred geographic landscape (tirtha), whereas Prabhas Patan is the modern coastal settlement associated with the Somnath sacred complex.

Trap 21: "Chalcolithic communities universally cultivated more cereals than Neolithic communities."

❌ Incorrect: Crop diversity and agricultural intensity varied drastically across regional ecosystems and phases; this cannot be generalized as an absolute subcontinental rule.

1. What Is the Chalcolithic Age? Concept & Chronological Horizon

The term Chalcolithic (derived from Greek chalcos = copper, lithos = stone) designates that technological stage in human history marked by the coexistence of copper metal implements alongside stone tools. In the chronology of the Indian subcontinent, it sits between the pure ground-stone Neolithic Age and the later metallurgical Bronze and Iron Ages.

Core Analytical Definition:

In the Indian context, the Chalcolithic does not represent an intermediate stepping stone on the way to urbanisation across the board. While the mature urban Harappan civilisation was technically a Bronze Age urban society, the term "Chalcolithic Cultures" in Indian archaeology refers specifically to the non-urban, non-Harappan, village-based regional farming and pastoral communities that flourished across Rajasthan, Central India, Gujarat, Maharashtra, and the Ganga Valley between c. 2500 BCE and 700 BCE.

Broad Textbook Chronological Ranges & Archaeological Caution:

Textbook chronologies offer useful reference brackets for the major cultures:

  • Kayatha Culture: c. 2450–2000 BCE (Strictly contemporary with Mature Harappan)
  • Ahar-Banas Culture: c. 2200–1500 BCE (Contemporary with late Mature & Post-Urban Harappan)
  • Savalda Culture: c. 2000–1800 BCE (Early Deccan Chalcolithic substrate)
  • Malwa Culture: c. 1700–1400 BCE (Post-Harappan central Indian efflorescence)
  • Jorwe Culture: c. 1400–700 BCE (Early Jorwe 1400–1000 BCE; Late Jorwe 1000–700 BCE)

Caution for Mains: These dates should be understood as broad conventional approximations. Regional archaeological horizons exhibited fluid lifespans, staggered beginnings, and long periods of overlapping interaction with neighboring groups.

1.1 Chalcolithic vs Bronze Age vs Iron Age — Fundamental Distinctions

Technological Stage Primary Metal & Metallurgy Settlement Scale & Economy Representative Indian Context
Neolithic Age No metal used; polished/ground stone celts, bone tools. Pioneered earliest sedentary village agriculture; egalitarian hamlets. Mehrgarh I, Burzahom, Lahuradewa, Utnur.
Chalcolithic Age Predominantly pure copper + continuing stone blades/microliths; bronze rare/occasional in late horizons; systematic alloying for utilitarian tools absent. Rural agro-pastoral villages; thatched wattle-and-daub huts; emerging chiefdoms. Ahar-Banas, Kayatha, Malwa, Jorwe (Inamgaon).
Bronze Age Bronze (Copper deliberately alloyed with ~10% tin or arsenic). Dense urban cities, planned brick architecture, craft guilds, script, long-distance trade. Harappan (Indus Valley) Civilisation.
Iron Age Smelted wrought iron and carbonized steel tools. Extensive agricultural surplus, dense hardwood forest clearance, Second Urbanisation. Painted Grey Ware (PGW), Megalithic South India, NBPW.
Writing & Urbanism Formulation (Mains Nuance): Avoid the simplistic assertion: "Chalcolithic people did not know writing and did not live in cities." The accurate academic formulation is: "Most non-Harappan Chalcolithic communities lacked a widespread script and were predominantly village-based, unlike the mature Harappan urban system." Furthermore, Chalcolithic communities did not invent village life; established sedentary village life was pioneered during the Neolithic.

2. General Socio-Economic & Material Features

Despite sharp regional variations, the Chalcolithic communities of the subcontinent shared several defining structural features:

2.1 Metallurgical & Tool Technology

  • First Metal Exploited: Copper was the first metal to be used in this technological sequence. Communities practiced true pyrotechnical smelting and metalworking—evident from slag, smelting furnaces, and crucibles—demonstrating knowledge of extracting copper from local ores, rather than merely cold-hammering native metal nuggets.
  • The Lithic Workhorse: The introduction of copper did not eliminate stone tools. Because copper was scarce, expensive, and relatively soft, everyday utilitarian operations (reaping crops, cutting skins, woodworking) relied on parallel-sided stone blades, micro-blades, and flakes made of fine siliceous stones (chert, chalcedony, agate, jasper).
  • Organic Implements: Bone points, antler tools, and wooden digging sticks tipped with stone rings (ring-stones) formed an indispensable part of the tool inventory.
  • Bronze in Chalcolithic Horizons (Important Nuance): Copper was the predominant metal alongside continuing stone technology, but the blanket claim that "bronze was completely absent from Chalcolithic cultures" is too absolute. Bronze objects and low-tin alloys have been documented in some regional prehistoric sequences, particularly towards later phases (most spectacularly demonstrated by the 60 kg Daimabad bronze hoard). The safe UPSC formulation is: "The Chalcolithic is characterised by the increasing use of copper alongside continuing stone technology; metal use varied by region and phase."

2.2 Agriculture, Crops & Agro-Pastoral Livelihood

Chalcolithic subsistence was predominantly mixed agro-pastoralism, where agricultural cultivation and animal husbandry were constantly supplemented by hunting, fishing, and gathering:

Catering Category Specific Crops & Regional Evidence UPSC Conceptual Notes
Cereals Wheat, barley, rice (in eastern and northern sites like Chirand, Pandu Rajar Dhibi), and regionally adapted millets. Crop diversity varied by region. Do not assume Chalcolithic universally cultivated more cereals than the Neolithic.
Millets Bajra (pearl millet) in Deccan horizons; Ragi (finger millet) in peninsular South. Essential drought-resilient crops suited to semi-arid Deccan plateau ecologies.
Pulses Lentil (masur), black gram (urad), green gram (mung), and grass pea (khesari). Provided vital protein balance in early agrarian diets (extensively documented at Navdatoli).
Cash / Fibre Crop Cotton seeds discovered in prehistoric horizons. Well-attested in prehistoric South Asian farming traditions; caution against restricting cotton solely to modern black-cotton soil regions.
Animal Husbandry Humped cattle (zebu), sheep, goats, buffaloes, and pigs. Cattle served dual roles: draft animals for tilling and carts, plus milk and meat.
Hunting & Fishing Venison, wild boar, turtle, water fowls, freshwater and marine fish. Fishhooks, harpoons, and faunal remains show active foraging. Eastern Chalcolithic communities did not live "solely on fish"—they were full agro-pastoralists.

2.3 Settlement Patterns & Domestic Architecture

  • Origins of Village Life (UPSC Trap): Villages did not begin for the first time during the Chalcolithic. Sedentary village farming was already pioneered during the Neolithic period (e.g., Mehrgarh, Lahuradewa). The Chalcolithic phase witnessed the further development, demographic expansion, and regional diversification of such agrarian village settlements.
  • Predominantly Village-Based: Settlements ranged from tiny pastoral hamlets (under 1 hectare) to regional nucleated centers (Inamgaon, Daimabad) reaching 5 to 20 hectares.
  • Diverse House Plans: Houses were constructed in rectangular, circular, and oval shapes. Multi-room dwellings were common, often combining rectangular living halls with circular storage rooms.
  • Construction Materials: Walls were built using mud plaster over reed screens (wattle-and-daub), thatched roofs supported by wooden posts, and sun-dried mud bricks.
  • Burnt-Brick Urban Architecture & Exception: Burnt-brick urban architecture was not a defining feature of most non-Harappan Chalcolithic settlements, which were generally village-based. However, it is incorrect to state categorically that Chalcolithic communities did not know burnt bricks at all—Gilund in Rajasthan preserves clear evidence of kiln-burnt brick structures.
  • Mature Jorwe Stability: In the mature Jorwe phase of Maharashtra, settlements became remarkably stable, uniform, and widespread across river basins before collapsing during the aridity crisis.

3. The Major Regional Chalcolithic Cultures in Detail

3.1 Ahar-Banas Culture (c. 2200–1500 BCE | Mewar, SE Rajasthan)

Ahar-Banas Culture Profile (Banas River Basin):

Centered in the Mewar region of south-eastern Rajasthan, the principal excavated sites include Ahar (type site), Gilund, and Balathal:

  • Ancient Name Tambavati: In ancient textual references, Ahar was known as Tambavati (meaning "a place rich in copper"), attesting to its rich metallurgical heritage.
  • Absence of Stone Microliths: While nearly all other Chalcolithic cultures used stone micro-blades, Ahar is uniquely characterized by the almost complete absence of stone microliths. The inhabitants utilized native copper from nearby Aravalli outcrops to manufacture copper axes, chisels, blades, and bangles directly from smelted copper.
  • Distinctive Pottery: Black-and-Red Ware (BRW) decorated with delicate, crisp white painted geometric patterns (dots, lines, chevrons).
  • Architectural Innovations: At Gilund, archaeologists unearthed stone-rubble and mud-brick structures alongside clear evidence of kiln-burnt bricks (extremely rare in non-Harappan Chalcolithic contexts). At Balathal, a massive mud and stone fortified enclosure was excavated. Balathal also yielded a human skeleton afflicted with leprosy dated to c. 2000 BCE—one of the earliest skeletal records of the disease in South Asia.

3.2 Kayatha Culture (c. 2450–2000 BCE | Malwa, Western MP)

Kayatha Culture Profile (Chhoti Kalisindh / Chambal Basin):

Located in Ujjain district, Madhya Pradesh, on the banks of the Chhoti Kalisindh river (a tributary of the Chambal), excavated by V.S. Wakankar and M.K. Dhavalikar:

  • Chronological Seniority: Radiocarbon dates place Kayatha between c. 2450 and 2000 BCE, proving it was strictly contemporary with the Mature Harappan phase.
  • Spectacular Metal & Bead Hoards: Inside a single mud house, excavators discovered a cache of 29 solid copper bangles stored in two pots, alongside two cast copper axes featuring sharp crescent-shaped cutting edges. Another jar contained thousands of fine steatite micro-beads, and two necklaces made of carnelian and agate.
  • Signature Pottery: Thick, sturdy red-slipped ware painted with chocolate or purple-brown designs, accompanied by an incised ware decorated with fine comb patterns.
  • Defensive Planning: Excavations also yielded evidence of early fortification and defensive ditch arrangements around the settlement.

3.3 Malwa Culture (c. 1700–1400 BCE | Central India, MP)

Malwa Culture Profile (Narmada, Betwa & Chambal Basins):

Distributed extensively across western Madhya Pradesh and parts of Maharashtra, with celebrated sites at Navdatoli, Maheshwar, Nagda, and Eran:

  • Navdatoli & Maheshwar (H.D. Sankalia): Situated on opposite banks of the Narmada river (Navdatoli on the south, Maheshwar on the north). Navdatoli revealed round (up to 4.5 m diameter) and rectangular wattle-and-daub houses with wooden posts, lime-plastered floors, and multiple hearths.
  • Unrivalled Agricultural Record: Navdatoli yielded the richest and most varied assemblage of food grains discovered at any prehistoric Indian site: wheat, barley, four varieties of pulses (green gram/mung, black gram/urad, grass pea/khesari, and lentil/masur), rice, linseed, and ber.
  • Eran Fortifications: Excavations at Eran (Bina river, Sagar district) revealed a large Chalcolithic settlement protected by a massive mud fortification wall and an artificial river-fed moat.
  • Pottery: Coarse buff- or cream-slipped ware with painted dark brown/black designs depicting geometric motifs, walking deer, peacocks, tigers, and dancing human figures.

3.4 Savalda Culture (c. 2000–1800 BCE | Dhule, Maharashtra)

Savalda Culture Profile (Tapi / Tapti Valley):

Identified at Savalda in Dhule district, Maharashtra, along the Tapi valley:

  • Earliest Deccan Horizon: Represents an important early Deccan Chalcolithic cultural tradition that predates the mature Jorwe culture.
  • Ceramic Hallmarks: Characterized by a distinctive thick ware with a chocolate-red to black painted slip over a pinkish-buff wash. The paintings uniquely depict weapons and tools (copper harpoons, barbed arrows, fishhooks) alongside natural fauna (peacocks, fishes) and geometric symbols.
  • Stratigraphic Importance: Forms the basal Chalcolithic layer at major Maharashtra multi-period sites like Daimabad and Prakash.

3.5 Jorwe Culture (c. 1400–700 BCE | Maharashtra / Northern Deccan)

Jorwe Culture Profile (Godavari, Pravara, Tapi & Bhima Basins):

The most extensive and best-documented Chalcolithic culture of peninsular India, with over 200 identified sites across Maharashtra, including Jorwe (type site), Inamgaon, Nevasa, Daimabad, Chandoli, and Prakash:

  • Riverine Network:
    • Prakash: Strategically situated in the fertile Tapi (Tapti) valley.
    • Daimabad & Nevasa: Located on the Pravara River (a primary tributary of the Godavari); Nevasa is an important multi-period site with Jorwe child urn burials and terracotta art.
    • Inamgaon: Located in the Bhima basin along the Ghod River.
  • Chronological Phases: Divided into Early Jorwe (c. 1400–1000 BCE), characterized by prosperous farming, and Late Jorwe (c. 1000–700 BCE), marked by severe climatic drying, impoverishment, and a retreat to pastoral nomadism.
  • Diagnostic Ceramic Style: Fine, wheel-turned black-on-red pottery with a dull matte finish. The diagnostic shapes include spouted jars with flaring funnel rims (lotas), stemmed goblets, and carinated bowls.

3.6 Inamgaon — The Definitive Benchmark Site (UPSC 2024 Prelims)

Inamgaon Deep-Dive (Pune District, Ghod River, Maharashtra):

Excavated over several seasons by H.D. Sankalia, M.K. Dhavalikar, and Z.D. Ansari, Inamgaon is the single most extensively excavated Chalcolithic settlement in India:

  • Settlement Hierarchy & The Chief's House: Over 130 mud houses were uncovered. While ordinary villagers lived in small single-room circular huts, the center of the settlement featured a massive five-room rectangular house containing a granary and an underground storage bin. Archaeologists interpret this as the residence of the village headman / chief.
  • Hydraulic Engineering: Villagers constructed a massive stone-and-mud diversion weir (dam) across the Ghod river and an irrigation canal (118 m long) to channel floodwaters into their fields.
  • Unique Burial Rites: Adults were buried in extended pits beneath house floors, aligned strictly North to South. Crucially, the feet were deliberately chopped off at the ankles—a superstitious ritual intended to prevent the deceased's spirit (ghost) from wandering and haunting the living.
  • Twin-Urn Child Burials: Deceased infants and children were placed inside two unbaked clay urns joined mouth-to-mouth and buried horizontally under the house floor.
  • Ritual Figurines: Excavators found an unbaked clay figurine of a headless female deity (Mother Goddess) associated with a clay figurine of a bull, hidden in a hole in a house floor.

3.7 Daimabad — Maharashtra's Largest Site & The Bronze Hoard

Daimabad Profile (Ahmednagar District, Pravara River, Maharashtra):
  • Scale: Spanning over 20 hectares, Daimabad was the largest Chalcolithic settlement in Maharashtra, demonstrating an unbroken stratigraphy: Savalda → Late Harappan → Malwa → Early Jorwe → Late Jorwe.
  • The Famous Daimabad Metal Hoard: In 1974, a local farmer unearthed four monumental solid-cast copper/bronze sculptures weighing more than 60 kg:
    1. Charioteer driving a two-wheeled chariot pulled by a pair of humped oxen (length: 45 cm; height: 16 cm).
    2. A magnificent standing Indian Rhinoceros on a wheeled platform.
    3. A solid-cast Elephant on a wheeled platform.
    4. A massive Water Buffalo with sweeping horns.
  • Archaeological Significance: The sculptures were cast using the cire perdue (lost-wax) technique. While their exact cultural attribution has been debated (Late Harappan vs Jorwe), they demonstrate extraordinary metallurgical mastery in prehistoric peninsular India.

3.8 Ganeshwar-Jodhpura Culture (c. 2800–2200 BCE | Aravallis, Rajasthan)

Geographical Location Located in Sikar and Jhunjhunu districts along the seasonal Kantli river, right next to the rich Baneshwar and Khetri copper belts.
Archaeological Finds Excavated by R.C. Agrawala; yielded over 1,000 copper implements: arrowheads, spearheads, fishhooks, celts, bangles, and chisels.
Feeder to Harappan Cities Operated as an autonomous raw copper extraction and supply base, funneling metal ingots and finished tools to Kalibangan and Indus sites.
Critical Distinction from Ahar-Banas Ganeshwar-Jodhpura must NOT be equated with Ahar-Banas. It was an independent hunting-pastoral complex with heavy microliths and incised orange ware.

3.9 Prabhas & Rangpur Cultures (c. 2000–1400 BCE | Gujarat)

Prabhas Patan & The Somnath Sacred Landscape:

Located on the coastal Kathiawar peninsula of Gujarat, Prabhas Patan is a celebrated archaeological site:

  • Ceramic Signature: Known for Lustrous Red Ware (LRW), a glossy burnished ceramic that evolved out of late Harappan pottery traditions.
  • Sacred Landscape Nuance: The site is closely associated with the modern Somnath sacred complex. UPSC Caution: Do not state that "Prabhas was simply the ancient name of Somnath" as a complete historical equivalence. Prabhas is the broader ancient sacred region (tirtha), whereas Prabhas Patan is the modern coastal settlement associated with it.
  • Rangpur (Bhadar River): Preserves an unbroken post-urban evolutionary sequence where Harappan pottery forms gradually adapted into the rural Lustrous Red Ware tradition.

3.10 Ochre Coloured Pottery (OCP) & The Mysterious Copper Hoards

The Upper Ganga-Yamuna Phenomenon:

Localized across western Uttar Pradesh, Haryana, eastern Rajasthan, and the Ganga-Yamuna Doab:

  • Ochre Coloured Pottery (OCP): A fragile, ill-fired, porous red ware with an ochre/orange-red slip that easily rubs off on contact with fingers. Found at Hastinapura, Atranjikhera, Lal Qila, and Saipai. The rolled, weathered surface is widely interpreted as evidence of prolonged waterlogging or massive prehistoric inundation of the Doab.
  • The Copper Hoard Horizon: Massive hoards of unalloyed copper tools buried in fields across Uttar Pradesh, Bihar, West Bengal, Odisha, and Madhya Pradesh. The largest single hoard was found at Gungeria (Balaghat district, MP), containing 424 heavy copper tools and 102 silver plates/discs!
  • Unique Tool Typology: Unlike Harappan tools, Copper Hoards feature distinct indigenous weapon forms: anthropomorphic figures (curved arm human figures, possibly boomerangs or religious emblems), antennae-hilted swords, barbed harpoons, hooked spearheads, and massive bar celts.
  • Saipai Excavation (Etawah, UP): Excavated by B.B. Lal in 1971; conclusively proved that OCP ceramics and Copper Hoard implements occur together in the same archaeological layer, demonstrating they belonged to the same culture.

3.11 Fortified Settlements & Emerging Settlement Hierarchy

Defensive Architecture in Chalcolithic India:

Evidence of fortifications and defensive works disproves the outdated notion that all Chalcolithic settlements were simple, peaceful, undefended hamlets:

Settlement State / Culture Defensive & Fortification Features
Kayatha Madhya Pradesh (Kayatha culture) Evidence of protective peripheral fortification ditch and defensive earthworks.
Eran Madhya Pradesh (Malwa culture) Massive mud rampart wall (approx. 5.6 m wide) accompanied by an artificial moat filled with river water.
Balathal Rajasthan (Ahar-Banas culture) Massive stone-and-mud fortified enclosure covering an area of 500 sq meters with corner bastions.
Inamgaon Maharashtra (Jorwe culture) Planned settlement with defensive perimeter ditch, embankment wall, and stone diversion weir.

GS-I Mains Significance: Fortifications provide concrete archaeological evidence for growing social complexity, organized communal labor mobilization, defense of grain surpluses against nomadic pastoralist raids, and inter-settlement conflict.

Because UPSC frequently pairs cultures with their characteristic ceramic wares in matching questions, this lookup table serves as the definitive reference:

⚠️ UPSC Pottery Trap & Core Correction:

The statement that Chalcolithic communities were the "first to use painted pottery in India" is too absolute and historically incorrect. Painted pottery already existed in pre-Harappan and Neolithic horizons (e.g., Mehrgarh II/III, Amri, Kot Diji). The correct UPSC formulation is: painted pottery became an indispensable diagnostic hallmark of Chalcolithic regional cultures, used by archaeologists to differentiate cultural groups.

Standard Chalcolithic Ceramic Repertoire: Common vessel forms across the cultures included bowls, dishes, jars, cups, basins, spouted vessels (lotas), stemmed chalices/cups, pedestalled bowls, dishes-on-stand, and large storage jars.

5. Burial Rites, Religious Symbolism & Social Differentiation

5.1 Burial Customs Across Regions

Burial practices varied considerably between regions, cultural phases, sites, and age groups, comprising extended burials, pit burials, and urn burials:

Region / Culture Burial Method Grave Orientation Grave Goods & Ritual Meaning
Maharashtra (Jorwe / Inamgaon) Extended pit burial under house floors for adults; feet chopped off at ankles. Twin-urn burial joined mouth-to-mouth for children. Predominantly North to South (head towards North). Clay pots, spouted jars, copper bangles, animal meat offerings. Amputating feet was a ritual superstition to immobilize the ghost.
Rajasthan (Ahar-Banas) Extended inhumation outside or inside the habitation; paved stone linings. Generally North to South or North-West to South-East. Accompanied by multiple black-and-red ware bowls and copper ornaments.
Central India (Malwa) Extended burials; some urn burials reported in late horizons. North-West to South-East orientation. Modest ceramic offerings; fewer metal grave goods compared to Jorwe.
⚠️ Burial Orientation Trap: A rigid formula such as "north-to-south in Maharashtra and east-to-west in South India" should not be memorized as an immutable universal law. Burial orientation and customs varied according to local archaeological contexts, phases, and age groups.
Interpretive Caution on Grave Goods: Pots, beads, or copper objects placed inside graves suggest beliefs concerning the treatment of the dead, ritual practices, or possible concepts of an afterlife. However, their exact theological meaning cannot be stated as definitively established.

5.2 Religious Imagery & Cultic Practices

Key Cultic Features:

  • Bull Cult: Terracotta figurines of humped bulls (zebu) are ubiquitous across Ahar, Kayatha, and Malwa, symbolizing pastoral fertility and male virility.
  • Female Figurines (Mother Goddess): Clay female figurines (both unbaked and baked) occur at Inamgaon, Nevasa, and Bilwali. Note the famous headless female figurine at Inamgaon found nestled with a clay bull inside a floor pit.
  • Fire Worship / Altars: Paved circular and rectangular clay enclosures containing charred animal bones, ash, and potsherds at Navdatoli and Inamgaon have been interpreted as domestic sacrificial fire pits.
  • Caution Against Over-Generalization (Mother Goddess Worship): The statement that female terracotta figurines conclusively prove institutionalized "Mother Goddess worship" is too categorical. In UPSC answers, use: "female figurines have been interpreted by some scholars as possible evidence of fertility or goddess-related cult practices."
  • Prehistoric Answer-Writing Language: For prehistoric religion, always prefer nuanced academic verbs ("suggests", "may indicate", "has been interpreted as") rather than presenting uncertain archaeological interpretations as dogmatic facts.

6. The Fatal Limitations of Chalcolithic Cultures (Mains Core)

Why Did the Chalcolithic Fail to Achieve Urbanisation? (RS Sharma Framework):

Despite domesticating animals, cultivating diverse crops, and manufacturing painted pottery, the non-Harappan Chalcolithic cultures never crossed the threshold into full urban civilization. Historian R.S. Sharma identified several fundamental technological, ecological, and biological constraints:

  1. Absence of Systematic Bronze Utilitarian Tools: While occasional prestige bronze objects are attested in later horizons (most famously the Daimabad bronze sculptures), non-Harappan Chalcolithic cultures lacked systematic tin-bronze metallurgy for everyday utilitarian tools and agricultural implements. Pure copper was too soft and scarce to fell dense hardwood forests or till hard soils, leaving them heavily reliant on brittle stone blades.
  2. Lack of Plow Agriculture: They did not possess the iron plowshare or heavy bronze plows. Farming was performed with digging sticks and stone celts, limiting cultivation to narrow river terraces and floodplains. They could not exploit the massive agrarian potential of the dense, sticky black cotton soil (Regur) of the Deccan interior.
  3. Devastating Infant Mortality: Excavations in Maharashtra (Inamgaon, Nevasa, Chandoli) revealed an appalling number of child urn burials. Archaeological demography indicates staggering child mortality, caused by recurring seasonal malnutrition, contaminated water, and epidemic infectious diseases in densely clustered mud villages.
  4. Absence of Writing: Despite their metallurgical and ceramic achievements, they never developed a script. The lack of writing prevented long-distance accounting, codified legal administration, and trade contracts.
  5. Climatic Vulnerability: When rainfall plummeted around 1200–1000 BCE, leading to severe aridification, the Jorwe settlements in Maharashtra could not sustain their agrarian base and collapsed.

Mains GS-I Model Question
"The Chalcolithic cultures of India were regionally differentiated agro-pastoral communities that exhibited technological sophistication yet failed to achieve urbanisation." Examine. (250 Words | 15 Marks)
Structured Answer Blueprint

Model Framework

• Introduction: Define the Chalcolithic phase (c. 2500–700 BCE); contrast its rural agrarian character with the contemporary urban Bronze Age Harappan civilisation.

• Regional Diversity: Briefly outline Ahar-Banas (copper smelting, BRW), Kayatha (bangle hoards), Malwa (Navdatoli cereal richness), Jorwe (Inamgaon settlement layout), and OCP/Copper Hoards.

• Technological Sophistication: Lost-wax bronze casting (Daimabad hoard), ceramic pyrotechnology, hydraulic canal/weir engineering at Inamgaon, craft specialization.

• Factors Inhibiting Urbanisation: Unalloyed pure copper (absence of bronze), reliance on stone micro-blades, inability to plow black cotton soil, high infant mortality, lack of script/accounting, and extreme vulnerability to ecological aridity (c. 1200 BCE).

• Conclusion: Rather than failed cities, Chalcolithic cultures should be evaluated as successful ecological adaptations that laid the indispensable agrarian and metallurgical foundation for the subsequent Iron Age Second Urbanisation.

9. 30-Second Memory Formula & Regional Master Chain

• Rajasthan: Ahar (Tambavati, no microliths, BRW with white paint, Gilund burnt brick, Balathal rampart) + Ganeshwar (Khetri copper feeder to Harappa; autonomous, NOT Ahar).

• Madhya Pradesh: Kayatha (Chambal, 29 copper bangles, chocolate slip, fortified) + Malwa (Navdatoli & Maheshwar on Narmada, cereal wealth, buff ware, Eran mud rampart & moat).

• Maharashtra: Savalda (Dhule, Tapi valley, early Deccan painted tools) → Jorwe (matte black-on-red, spouted lota) + Inamgaon (Ghod river/Bhima basin, 5-room chief's house, weir, adult burials with feet chopped off) + Daimabad & Nevasa (Pravara/Godavari, 20 ha, 60 kg bronze hoard) + Prakash (Tapi valley).

• Gujarat: Prabhas Patan (Somnath sacred tirtha landscape) + Rangpur (Bhadar river) → Lustrous Red Ware (LRW) post-urban continuity.

• Ganga-Yamuna Doab: OCP + Copper Hoards (anthropomorphic figures, antennae swords, harpoons, Saipai co-occurrence, Gungeria 424 copper tools).

• Core Exam Rules: Copper + stone blades (copper did NOT eliminate stone); bronze was NOT 100% absent (Daimabad hoard); Neolithic founded earliest villages; Chalcolithic NOT first painted pottery; female figurines = fertility/ritual interpretation (NOT dogmatic Mother Goddess religion); ended by c. 1200–1000 BCE drought.

🔥 High-Value Chalcolithic & Prehistoric Corrections

Earlier Simplification / Common Myth UPSC-Safe Correct Version
Bronze was completely absent from all Chalcolithic contexts. Metal assemblages varied regionally; some later prehistoric contexts contain bronze (e.g. 60 kg Daimabad hoard) as well as copper.
Chalcolithic communities founded the first Indian villages. Village life already existed during the Neolithic; the Chalcolithic phase expanded and regionally diversified settlements.
Chalcolithic people did not know burnt bricks at all. Extensive baked-brick architecture was not characteristic of most non-Harappan villages, but Gilund preserves clear kiln-burnt brick structures.
Burial orientation was strictly uniform across India. There is no single universal rule regarding burial orientation; practices varied by site, age group, and cultural phase.
Female figurines prove institutionalized Mother Goddess worship. Female figurines are archaeological evidence whose religious interpretation remains uncertain (possible fertility/cultic practices).
Black-and-Red Ware = only Late Harappan. Black-and-Red Ware is widespread across multiple cultures and periods (Ahar-Banas, Neolithic Koldihwa, Megalithic, Iron Age).
