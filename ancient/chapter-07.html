CHAPTER 7

Harappan Culture: Bronze Age Urbanisation

The Indus Civilisation — Mature Phase (c. 2600–1900 BCE), Grid Town Planning, Dholavira Water Engineering, Lothal Maritime Trade, Steatite Seals, Undeciphered Script, Rakhigarhi 2026 Excavations & Multi-Causal Transformation

UPSC Relevance & Analytical Framework

UPSC Core Perspective:

The Harappan Civilisation (Indus Civilisation) marks South Asia's First Urbanisation and one of the three primary Old World civilisations alongside Mesopotamia and Egypt. For UPSC CSE, it is not merely a catalogue of antique artefacts, but a profound study in urban spatial organisation, civic public infrastructure, pyrotechnical craft specialisation, long-distance trade geopolitics, and socio-ecological resilience.

UPSC repeatedly examines Harappa across six testing axes: (1) Site-Feature Diagnostic Matching (e.g. Dholavira water engineering, Kalibangan ploughed field), (2) Material Technology (lost-wax bronze casting, stone blades, complete absence of iron), (3) Rigvedic vs. Harappan Comparative Traps (horse, armour, cities, religion), (4) Civic Architecture (Great Bath, standardised 1:2:4 bricks, underground drainage), (5) Methodological Restraint (interpreting religion/polity without a deciphered script), and (6) Multi-Causal Transformation (climate aridification, river shifts, and de-urbanisation rather than sudden extinction).

Archaeological Discovery & Nomenclature Nuance:

In academic archaeology, the term "Harappan Civilisation" is preferred over "Indus Valley Civilisation" because the culture's geographical distribution extends far beyond the Indus river basin into Haryana, Rajasthan, Gujarat, western Uttar Pradesh, Jammu, Maharashtra (Daimabad), and northern Afghanistan (Shortughai). By archaeological convention, a culture is named after its type-site—the first locality where its diagnostic assemblage was discovered.

  • Daya Ram Sahni (1921): Conducted the first systematic excavations at Harappa in the Montgomery (Sahiwal) district of Punjab.
  • R. D. Banerji (1922): Recognised and excavated the ancient ruins at Mohenjo-daro in Larkana, Sindh, establishing that the two far-flung settlements belonged to the same previously unknown civilisation.
  • Sir John Marshall (1924): Director-General of the Archaeological Survey of India (ASI), made the dramatic public international announcement in the Illustrated London News, coining the term "Indus Valley Civilization".

High-Yield Prelims Focus — Key Facts & Trap Zone

1. Common Prelims Trap Zone — Scrutinize Absolute Statements

Trap 1: "Mature Harappan people used iron weapons, axes, and armour." (UPSC 2012, 2017)

❌ Incorrect & Fatal Error: The Harappan Civilisation was strictly a Bronze Age culture. They utilized copper, bronze (copper-tin/arsenic alloy), gold, silver, and lead. Iron was completely unknown in the Mature Harappan phase and only appeared in the Indian subcontinent around ~1000 BCE in the subsequent Iron Age / PGW / Megalithic horizons.

Trap 2: "The Harappan script has been successfully deciphered by linguists."

❌ Incorrect: The Harappan script remains strictly undeciphered. Claims of decipherment (linking it to Dravidian, Proto-Sanskrit, or Munda) represent unproven individual scholarly hypotheses outside the scientific consensus. Because no bilingual inscription (like Egypt's Rosetta Stone) exists, its linguistic affiliation remains unknown.

Trap 3: "The horse was a central domestic animal and war asset in Harappan urban life." (UPSC 2013, 2017)

❌ Incorrect: The horse was not a defining or characteristic feature of Harappan culture. The horse-drawn war chariot is a hallmark of the later Vedic culture. While disputed equine skeletal remains/teeth were reported from Surkotada (Gujarat), their identification as true domesticated Equus caballus is heavily debated among archaeozoologists. The typical Harappan animals on seals are the unicorn, humped zebu bull, elephant, rhinoceros, and tiger.

Trap 4: "Harappan cities were dominated by monumental royal palaces and magnificent religious temples." (UPSC 2013)

❌ Incorrect: Unlike contemporary Mesopotamia (Ziggurats) and Egypt (Pyramids, Karnak temples), not a single identifiable temple or royal palace has been excavated at any Harappan site. The civilisation was characterized by monumental civic infrastructure (baths, drains, granaries, water reservoirs) rather than autocratic monuments.

Trap 5: "All Harappan settlements followed an identical dual layout of a Western Citadel and an Eastern Lower Town."

❌ Incorrect: Town planning exhibited striking regional diversity:
• Dholavira (Gujarat): Split into three distinct fortified divisions (Citadel / Castle, Middle Town, and Lower Town).
• Lothal & Surkotada: Enclosed within a single continuous perimeter wall with internal compartmentalisation.
• Chanhudaro: The only major Harappan city completely lacking a citadel.

Trap 6: "Every Harappan street intersected at exact 90-degree right angles in a perfect grid."

❌ Incorrect: While major urban centers like Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, and Kalibangan display impressive grid layouts, settlement designs were adapted to local topographies. Banawali (Haryana) lacked a systematic right-angle grid and instead featured radial streets emanating from a fortified enclosure.

Trap 7: "The ploughed field evidence was discovered at Harappa or Mohenjo-daro."

❌ Incorrect: The iconic criss-cross ploughed agricultural field with perpendicular furrows (evidence of two crops grown simultaneously, like mustard and chickpea) was excavated at Kalibangan in Rajasthan in an Early Harappan layer.

Trap 8: "Female terracotta figurines conclusively prove a universal, state-administered Mother Goddess religion."

❌ Incorrect: Prehistoric religion requires disciplined archaeological restraint. Female terracotta figurines are abundant in Sindh and Punjab (Mohenjo-daro, Harappa) but are virtually absent at major sites in Rajasthan (Kalibangan) and Gujarat (Lothal). They reflect localized fertility or household cultic practices rather than a monolithic state church.

Trap 9: "The Pashupati Seal provides textual confirmation of Lord Shiva's worship."

❌ Incorrect: The label "Proto-Shiva / Pashupati" was coined by Sir John Marshall based on stylistic comparison with later Hindu iconography (tricephalic seated yogic figure surrounded by animals). Because the accompanying script cannot be read, this remains an archaeological hypothesis, not a deciphered theological doctrine.

Trap 10: "The brick dockyard at Lothal is an uncontested, indisputable modern naval harbour."

❌ Incorrect: While S.R. Rao and standard NCERTs interpret the baked-brick basin at Lothal as a tidal dockyard, alternative archaeo-engineering interpretations suggest it may have served as a large freshwater irrigation tank or drinking reservoir. In Mains, present the dockyard as a leading, highly probable archaeological interpretation.

Trap 11: "The Harappan Civilisation was violently destroyed in 1900 BCE by invading Vedic Aryans."

❌ Incorrect: Mortimer Wheeler's 1947 hypothesis ("Indra stands accused" based on skeletons at Mohenjo-daro) has been completely refuted by modern forensics and stratigraphy. Skeletons were from different geological layers and show no battle trauma. The decline was a prolonged, multi-causal ecological process of climate aridification, river shifts, and regional de-urbanisation.

Trap 12: "Standardised bricks and weights prove the existence of an autocratic central emperor."

❌ Incorrect: Uniform 1:2:4 brick proportions and binary-decimal stone weights demonstrate exceptional civic coordination, commercial conventions, and inter-regional integration, but offer zero evidence for a single autocratic monarch. Political power may have been held by merchant oligarchies, municipal councils, or regional city-states.

Trap 13: "Mehrgarh in Balochistan was a mature urban Harappan metropolis."

❌ Incorrect: Mehrgarh is NOT a Mature Harappan city. It is an early Neolithic and Chalcolithic farming village (c. 7000–2600 BCE) that provided the deep antecedent technological and agricultural foundation upon which the Indus civilisation later developed.

Trap 14: "Cotton cultivation was invented by the Mature Harappans."

❌ Incorrect: While the Harappans were the first in the Old World to produce spun cotton textiles on a commercial scale (Greeks called cotton Sindon, derived from Sindh), the earliest archaeological trace of cotton seeds in South Asia comes from Neolithic Mehrgarh Period II (~5000 BCE).

Trap 15: "Sohgaura in Uttar Pradesh was a major Harappan coastal port." (UPSC 2019)

❌ Incorrect: Sohgaura is NOT a Harappan site. It is famous for a Mauryan-period copper-plate inscription written in Ashokan Prakrit concerning royal drought relief granaries. Always verify: Site + Period + State + Characteristic Finding.

1. Chronological Horizons & Evolutionary Stages

The Harappan Civilisation was not an overnight miracle imported from West Asia, but the organic culmination of a multi-millennial socio-economic evolution rooted in the indigenous agro-pastoral communities of South Asia. Archaeologists classify this unbroken continuum into three primary phases:

Mains Analytical Perspective — Gradual Transition vs. Diffusionism:

Early colonial historians (e.g. Mortimer Wheeler, Gordon Childe) posited that the idea of civilization diffused into the Indus valley from Sumerian Mesopotamia. Modern archaeology (Rafique Mughal's Hakra surveys, Jean-François Jarrige's work at Mehrgarh, Jim Shaffer) has conclusively dismantled this myth:

  • The transition from Early to Mature Harappan was an internal, indigenous transformation driven by intensification of agriculture in the floodplains, craft specialization, and expanding internal trade networks.
  • The standardized Mature Harappan cultural toolkit (1:2:4 bricks, chert blades, steatite seals) was an indigenous integration of varied regional technologies.

2. Geographical Extent & Cardinal Outposts

The Mature Harappan civilisation encompassed a triangular/trapezoidal territory spanning approximately 1.3 million square kilometers—an expanse substantially larger than contemporary Bronze Age Egypt and Mesopotamia combined!

The Four Cardinal Outposts (The Harappan Compass):
  • Northernmost Limit: Manda on the right bank of the Chenab River, foot of the Pir Panjal range, Jammu & Kashmir.
  • Southernmost Limit: Daimabad on the Pravara River (Godavari basin), Ahmednagar district, Maharashtra (Late Harappan horizon).
  • Westernmost Limit: Sutkagen Dor on the Dasht River, Makran Coast, Balochistan (Pakistan–Iran border region).
  • Easternmost Limit: Alamgirpur on the Hindon River (Yamuna basin), Meerut district, western Uttar Pradesh.

Quick Formula: Manda (N) ↔ Daimabad (S) | Sutkagen Dor (W) ↔ Alamgirpur (E)

Isolated Northern Trading Enclave: Shortughai (Afghanistan):

Beyond the contiguous territorial block, the Harappans established an isolated, permanent trading colony at Shortughai in northern Afghanistan along the Oxus (Amu Darya) river. Shortughai featured typical Harappan mud-brick architecture, seals, lapis lazuli beads, and irrigation canal networks, functioning as an extraterritorial procurement depot for Badakhshan lapis lazuli, tin, and camel transport.

3. Town Planning, Civic Architecture & Water Engineering

Urban planning is the single most celebrated hallmark of the Harappan civilisation and a frequent theme in UPSC GS-I Mains (2014, 2025).

3.1 The Grid Layout & Spatial Zoning

  • The Chessboard / Gridiron Pattern: Cities were divided into rectangular blocks by wide, straight main avenues intersecting approximately at right angles (cardinal North-South and East-West alignment). Secondary lanes fed into these arterial streets.
  • Functional Dual Zoning (The Classic Model): Most major sites (Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Kalibangan) were organized into two distinct fortified sectors:
    1. The Western Citadel (Acropolis): Built upon an artificially raised massive mud-brick platform (up to 12 meters high). It housed public monumental buildings, assembly halls, the Great Bath, granaries, and the civic elite. Protected by substantial fortification walls and bastions.
    2. The Eastern Lower Town: Larger in area, situated at a lower elevation. It accommodated the residential quarters of ordinary citizens, artisans' workshops, commercial bazaars, and merchant dwellings.
  • Regional Variations (Non-Universal Planning):
    • Dholavira: Exceptionally divided into three distinct fortified sections: the Citadel (Castle + Bailey), the Middle Town (for administrative officials), and the Lower Town.
    • Lothal & Surkotada: Did not feature two separate detached mounds; both the acropolis and lower town were integrated inside a single common perimeter rampart.
    • Chanhudaro: Featured no citadel whatsoever; it was an open, unfortified industrial township.

3.2 Standardised Building Materials & Construction Precision

  • The Standard Brick Proportion: Both sun-dried mud bricks (for core platforms and ramparts) and kiln-baked bricks (for drains, bathrooms, and exterior walls) adhered strictly to the standardized volumetric ratio of 1 : 2 : 4 (Thickness : Width : Length; typically 7 cm × 14 cm × 28 cm for houses, and 10 cm × 20 cm × 40 cm for city ramparts).
  • English Bond Masonry: Bricks were laid in alternate courses of headers and stretchers using fine mud mortar or gypsum-sand plaster, providing immense structural stability against seismic shocks.
  • Stone Architecture at Dholavira: While Indus cities relied almost exclusively on baked brick due to alluvial silt availability, Dholavira utilized dressed sandstone rubble masonry for monumental gateways, pillars, and reservoirs.

3.3 The World's Earliest Underground Drainage Network

Civic Sanitation & Hygiene Standards:

No other Bronze Age civilisation—neither Egypt nor Mesopotamia—paid such meticulous attention to public sanitation:

  • Household to Street Drains: Every domestic courtyard or bathroom had an unpaved or paved bathing platform that sloped toward an aperture in the outer wall. Wastewater drained into covered terra-cotta pipes or brick channels running beneath the street pavement.
  • Domestic Latrine Installations: Several houses at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro featured commode/latrine-like installations—sloping brick seats connected by terracotta chutes through the outer wall into large ceramic pots or soakage pits outside.
  • Inspection Manholes & Maintenance: Street drains were covered with flat, removable baked-brick slabs or dressed limestone blocks that municipal sanitation workers could lift for periodic desilting.
  • Sump Pits (Soakage Jars): Drains featured corbelled brick drop-traps and large earthen soak jars placed at intervals to catch solid waste, ensuring that only clear liquid wastewater flowed into the arterial drains emptying outside the city perimeter.
Prelims Diagnostic Angle — Trace the Sanitation Hierarchy: Harappan sanitation involved more than a single city-wide drain. Think in sequence: Household bathing/washing area → Private/local drain or wall chute → Domestic soakage/sump/pottery jars → Covered municipal street drainage, with considerable variation adapted to local topographies.

3.4 Water Architecture — The Great Bath & Dholavira's Reservoirs

The Great Bath (Mohenjo-daro) A monumental sunken rectangular swimming tank measuring 11.88 m × 7.01 m × 2.43 m depth. Built on the Citadel mound, accessed by two flights of brick stairs on the North and South. The floor and walls were made water-tight using finely fitted baked bricks laid on edge with gypsum mortar, backed by a 3-cm layer of natural bitumen (asphalt). Surrounded on three sides by a portico of rooms and private changing cubicles. Filled from a massive brick-lined well and drained through a corbelled arched culvert. Universally interpreted as an installation for ritual, purificatory elite ablutions.
Reservoir Hydraulics (Dholavira) In the arid, hyper-saline island of Khadir in the Rann of Kutch, Dholavira's engineers created a breathtaking hydrological system. They constructed stone check-dams across two seasonal streams (Manhar and Mansar) to divert monsoon floodwaters through stone-cut canals into a ring of 16 massive rock-cut and masonry reservoirs surrounding the city. The reservoirs stored up to 300,000 cubic meters of fresh water—a masterclass in ancient rainwater harvesting and climate adaptation.

3.5 Storage Facilities & The "Granary" Terminology Caution

UPSC Terminology Caution — Use "Granary" Carefully:

Large monumental brick structures at Harappa (the "Great Granary" comprising 12 units in 2 rows on a raised revetted platform) and Mohenjo-daro have traditionally been identified in textbooks as state granaries. However, contemporary archaeologists (J.M. Kenoyer, Gregory Possehl, Shereen Ratnagar) urge caution:

  • No grain impressions, charred cereal remains, or pollen have been excavated within the floor cavities of these structures.
  • Their precise functional purpose remains debated—they may have served as state storehouses, textile/goods warehouses, or civic meeting halls.
  • Mains Writing Best Practice: Phrasing such as "structures traditionally interpreted as granaries or storage facilities" is academically sound and guards against dogmatic assertions.

3.6 Political Organisation & Administrative Governance

Deconstructing the "Merchant Oligarchy" Myth:

Coaching manuals frequently assert that Harappa was "ruled by a merchant class". In UPSC CSE, avoid treating this as an established fact:

  • Absence of Direct Evidence: Because the Indus script is undeciphered, names of rulers, government offices, and constitutional frameworks remain entirely unknown.
  • Indirect Inference of Administration: The extraordinary standardisation of bricks (1:2:4), metrological weights, municipal drainage layouts, and flood defense platforms proves the existence of robust civic coordination and central administrative authority.
  • Leading Scholarly Models:
    • Single Empire: Unlikely given marked regional ceramic and mortuary variations.
    • Network of City-States: Prominent urban centers (Harappa, Mohenjo-daro, Rakhigarhi, Dholavira) likely functioned as regional capitals governed by municipal councils, merchant guilds, or elite lineages (Kenoyer).
  • Mains Model Formulation: "Harappan political organisation can be inferred only indirectly from urban coordination, standardisation, craft regulation and monumental public works. The precise nature of kingship, bureaucracy and the role of merchants remains uncertain."

4. Domestic Architecture & Everyday Household Life

Excavations of residential blocks in the Lower Towns of Mohenjo-daro, Harappa, and Lothal provide rich insights into family life and social values:

  • The Central Courtyard (Atrium): Houses were designed around an open inner courtyard, with 4 to 12 rooms arranged around its perimeter. The courtyard was the social nucleus of the home, used for cooking, weaving, and spinning.
  • Maniac Obsession with Privacy:
    • Entrance doorways were positioned in narrow side lanes rather than directly facing the bustling main street.
    • Ground-floor outer walls facing the street were completely windowless, preventing passersby from peeking inside.
    • Entrances were constructed with screening walls or angled corridors, so that opening the front door did not allow a direct line of sight into the central courtyard.
  • Upper Storeys & Staircases: Many houses possessed thick ground-floor walls and surviving brick staircases, proving the existence of second storeys or accessible rooftop terraces.
  • Domestic Water & Bathrooms: Nearly every private house contained a paved bathing platform with terracotta water drains. Mohenjo-daro alone contained an estimated 700 private brick-lined wells.
  • Social Stratification Visible in Housing: Domestic housing ranged from modest, two-room barrack-like quarters (such as the workmen's quarters at Harappa) to sprawling palatial mansions covering several hundred square meters with private wells, multiple courtyards, and servant quarters.

5. Agrarian Economy, Food Surplus & Animal Husbandry

Dense urban cities were sustained by a massive, highly productive agricultural hinterland exploiting the annual inundations of the Indus and Saraswati-Ghaggar river systems. The Harappan cultural zone covered a remarkably diverse ecological mosaic—including alluvial plains, semi-arid plateaus, uplands, coastal deltas, and riverine environments. Subsistence was strategically diversified through agriculture, pastoralism, hunting, fishing, and gathering, insulating settlements against localized crop failures.

5.1 Crop Repertoire, Seasonal Cropping & Agricultural Technology

Seasonal Cropping (Rabi & Kharif Dynamics): Harappan cultivation included both winter (Rabi) and summer (Kharif) crops. In alluvial floodplains, winter crops (wheat, barley, peas, chickpeas, lentils) were sown after seasonal floodwaters receded in autumn and harvested in spring. Avoid rigid textbook assertions that all Harappans universally planted in November and harvested in April—cropping schedules were adapted to regional precipitation and river inundation regimes.

5.2 The Ploughed Field of Kalibangan & Banawali Plough Model

Discovery of the World's Earliest Ploughed Field (B.B. Lal & B.K. Thapar):

Excavated in the pre-Harappan / Early Harappan levels at Kalibangan (Rajasthan), the site preserved a fossilized field surface with criss-cross intersecting furrows:

  • One set of furrows was spaced 30 cm apart (running East-West), while a perpendicular set was spaced 1.9 meters apart (running North-South).
  • This archaeological breakthrough proves that the Harappans practiced simultaneous dual-cropping on the same field (the wider furrows for taller crops like mustard, the closer furrows for shorter legumes like chickpea)—an exact agricultural technique suited to raising different crops in the same field and still practiced in rural Rajasthan today!
  • Plough Technology: Terracotta toy models of plows have been excavated at Banawali (Haryana) and Jawaiwala (Cholistan), confirming the use of wooden plows drawn by oxen.

5.3 Irrigation, Floodwater Management & The Shortughai Canal Evidence

UPSC Nuance — Were Canals Used in Harappan Agriculture?

It is an error to claim that the Harappans had a uniform pan-Indian canal irrigation system. Water management varied greatly with regional ecology:

  • Floodwater & Wells: In the Indus alluvium, agriculture relied heavily on annual river inundations, residual flood moisture, and brick-lined groundwater wells. Embankments (gabarbands) in Balochistan trapped hill runoff.
  • Engineered Canals at Shortughai (Afghanistan): Henri-Paul Francfort's excavations at Shortughai on the Oxus (Amu Darya) river revealed archaeological remains of an engineered irrigation canal system tapping the Kokcha River to bring water onto elevated agricultural terraces.
  • Best UPSC Formulation: "Harappan water management included regionally adapted strategies such as floodwater cultivation, wells, reservoirs (Dholavira), check-dams, channels and, at Shortughai, clear archaeological evidence of canal irrigation. Avoid the absolute statement that canal irrigation was either universally present or universally absent."

5.4 Animal Husbandry, Beasts of Burden & Aquatic Resources

  • Domesticated Livestock: The backbone of pastoral economy was the humped zebu cattle (Bos indicus), prized for draft labor, milk, and meat. Buffaloes, sheep, goats, pigs, dogs, and domestic cats were widely reared.
  • Traction & Beasts of Burden: Humped oxen and buffaloes provided traction for plows and heavy two-wheeled carts. Camels (skeletal remains at Kalibangan) and donkeys / wild asses were used as pack animals across arid transport corridors.
  • Aquatic and Marine Resource Exploitation:
    • Harappan coastal and riverine communities extensively harvested marine catfish, molluscs, and river fish for diet and shell crafting.
    • Fish bones and marine shell remains at inland urban centers like Harappa indicate that dried, salted fish and marine commodities were transported hundreds of kilometres inland via riverboats and caravan networks.
  • The Horse Debate in UPSC:
    • Unlike the later Vedic Aryans who were horse-centric, the Harappans were an ox/bull-centric civilisation.
    • While J.P. Joshi reported horse bones and teeth from Surkotada (c. 2000 BCE), international palaeontologists (e.g. Richard Meadow) dispute this, identifying them as remains of the wild ass (Equus hemionus) or onager.
    • Safe UPSC Formulation: Horse evidence in Mature Harappan contexts is limited, controversial, and atypical; the horse was NOT a regular, widespread, or militarily deployed animal in Harappan society.

6. Specialized Crafts, Metallurgy & Pyrotechnology

Harappan cities were bustling manufacturing powerhouses where advanced pyrotechnical mastery produced luxury goods for both domestic elite consumption and international export.

6.1 Metallurgy & The Lost-Wax Method (Cire Perdue)

  • Bronze Casting Masterpiece — The Dancing Girl: Excavated at Mohenjo-daro by Ernest Mackay (1926), this 10.5-cm bronze statuette was cast using the advanced lost-wax (cire perdue) technique:
    • A wax model was sculpted, coated with fine clay, and heated so the wax melted away; molten bronze was then poured into the hollow clay mould.
    • Stylistically, the statuette depicts a young girl in an audacious, self-confident tribhanga dancing posture, naked except for a necklace, her right hand resting casually on her hip and her entire left arm covered with 24 bangles made of bone/shell.
  • Utilitarian Metal Products: Flat axes, chisels, knives, saws with undulating teeth, spears, fishhooks, and arrowheads manufactured from copper alloyed with tin (to produce bronze) or arsenic (arsenical bronze).
  • Precious Metals: Gold beads and pendants (sourced from Kolar, Karnataka); silver vessels and ornaments (purified from argentiferous galena); electrum (natural alloy of gold and silver).
  • COMPLETE ABSENCE OF IRON: The Harappans had zero knowledge of iron smelting.

6.2 The Bead-Making Industry — Chanhudaro & Lothal

Industrial Bead Factories:

Specialized bead workshops complete with flues, heating kilns, stone anvils, and copper-tipped micro-drills were unearthed at Chanhudaro (Sindh) and Lothal (Gujarat):

  • Carnelian Pyrotechnology: Raw brown nodules of agate/carnelian from the Ratanpur mines (Rajpipla, Gujarat) were repeatedly baked in specialized kilns to develop a deep, lustrous translucent red hue.
  • Etched Carnelian Beads: Designs (white concentric circles, zig-zags) were etched onto the polished red carnelian using an alkaline paste made of washing soda, then fired to bond permanently into the stone matrix. These etched beads were luxury exports found in royal Mesopotamian graves at Ur and Kish!
  • Micro-Steatite Beads: Millions of microscopic disc beads (measuring less than 1 mm in diameter) were produced from talc/steatite paste—a feat of nanotechnological precision in the ancient world.

6.3 Marine Shell-Working & Faience

  • Shell Manufacturing Centers (Nageshwar & Balakot): Situated right on the Arabian Sea coast, these sites specialized exclusively in cutting the marine conch shell (Turbinella pyrum) to manufacture bangles, ladles, saucers, and inlay plaques for furniture.
  • Faience Craft: A composite material created by mixing crushed silica sand, flux, and copper ore colorant, shaped into moulds and fired at high temperatures to create a vitreous, glossy turquoise-blue surface (used for bangles, amulets, and perfume scent bottles).
  • Pottery: Characterized by wheel-thrown sturdy Red Ware with black painted decorations (often called Black-on-Red Ware). Common motifs include pipal leaves, intersecting circles, peacocks, fish scales, and geometric cross-hatchings. Common vessel shapes included dishes-on-stand, S-shaped jars, perforated strainers (for brewing beverages), and pointed-base goblets.

7. Seals, Sealing System & Standardised Weights

Standardisation across an empire-sized territory is the clearest index of Harappan administrative efficiency.

7.1 Steatite Seals — The Badges of Mercantile Identity

  • Form & Material: Over 2,000 seals have been excavated, overwhelmingly square or rectangular tablets (typically 2 cm × 2 cm) manufactured from soft, fine-grained steatite (soapstone), carved in intaglio and treated with an alkaline coating before being fired to a lustrous white.
  • Reverse Boss: The back of the seal featured a perforated perforated hemispherical boss, through which a cord was strung to wear it around the merchant's neck or wrist.
  • Iconographic Motifs:
    • The Unicorn: A mythical, one-horned bovine creature with a single curved horn, accompanied by a ritual incense burner or feeding manger—by far the most frequent motif (over 60% of all animal seals).
    • Other prominent motifs: Humped zebu bull, elephant, tiger, rhinoceros, water buffalo, goat, crocodile (gharial), and chimerical human-animal hybrids.
    • Noticeably absent from seals: The cow, the horse, and the domestic camel.
  • Functional Purpose: Seals were stamped onto wet clay patties pressed over ropes tied around merchant baggage (clay sealings or bullae). Discoveries of clay impressions bearing sealings on one side and woven reed-mat textures on the other (found inside the Lothal warehouse) prove their role in authenticating ownership and preventing cargo tampering.
Seal Dimension Diagnostic Archaeological Feature UPSC Prelims & Mains Utility
Material Steatite (Soapstone) is the overwhelmingly characteristic material; seals and sealings also occur in agate, chert, copper, faience, and terracotta. Eliminate options claiming seals were made exclusively of metal or baked clay.
Shape & Typology Square stamp seals (typically 2 × 2 cm) are most common; rectangular/oblong cylindrical seals also occur. Distinguish from Mesopotamian cylindrical roller seals.
Design Composition Typical seals combine an animal motif in intaglio with a short Indus inscription carved above the animal figure. Standard format across sites; unicorn motif accounts for over 60%.

7.2 Metrological Precision & Internal Exchange Networks

Chert Cubical Weights & Exchange Mechanism Nuance:

Harappan exchange was regulated by a strictly standardized metrological system made from polished cubical chert stones:

  • Dual System: The lower denominations followed a binary progression: 1, 2, 4, 8, 16, 32, 64... up to 12,800. The 16th unit was the primary standard benchmark, weighing approximately 13.64 grams (remarkably close to the traditional Indian tola / 16 annas!).
  • Decimal System: The higher denominations progressed in a decimal system: 100, 200, 500, 1000, 10,000.
  • Measuring Scales: Linear measurement was attested by finely incised measuring rods made of ivory (Lothal) and shell (Mohenjo-daro).
  • Nuance on "Barter System": Older textbooks describe internal exchange simply as a "barter economy". A more rigorous academic formulation is that distribution operated through measured quantities, state/civic redistribution, and reciprocal trade. While coins did not exist, transactions were strictly metrologically quantified via standardised weights.

8. The Harappan Script & The Dholavira Signboard

Epigraphical Realities & UPSC Best Formulation:
  • Undeciphered Mystery: The script remains completely undeciphered. There are no long historical texts, royal decrees, or bilingual inscriptions (like Egypt's Rosetta Stone) available.
  • Writing Direction: The general, standard direction of writing is strictly Right to Left (evident from the cramping of signs at the left margin where scribes ran out of room). Occasional multi-line inscriptions show boustrophedon (alternating right-to-left and left-to-right), but boustrophedon was rare and not the normal rule.
  • Best Exam Statement: "The Indus script remains undeciphered and is generally written from right to left; occasional inscriptions show other directional arrangements, including rare boustrophedon examples."
  • Sign Corpus: Contains between 400 and 450 distinct signs / glyphs. Because this is too many for an alphabet (~26 signs) and too few for a pure logographic script (~thousands of signs), linguists classify it as a logo-syllabic script (each sign representing a word or syllable).
  • Brevity: Inscriptions are notoriously brief, averaging just 5 signs per seal. The longest single continuous Harappan inscription contains only 26 signs (found on a three-sided molded tablet from Mohenjo-daro).
  • The Most Frequent Glyph: The stylized fish symbol (interpreted by Asko Parpola as representing stars/divinities using Dravidian homophones).
The Monumental Dholavira Inscription (The Signboard):

In 1990, R.S. Bisht excavated an extraordinary epigraphical discovery at Dholavira: fallen in the debris of the Western Gateway of the Citadel lay a wooden board containing 10 gigantic Indus signs:

  • Each sign was approximately 37 cm high and 25 to 27 cm wide, meticulously fashioned out of inlaid crystalline minerals (white gypsum / baked faience).
  • The wooden backing had rotted away, but the mineral inlay remained intact. It functioned as a civic or royal signboard hanging over the gateway to announce the authority of the city.

10. Religious Life, Cultic Practices & Funerary Rites

Methodological Caution on Prehistoric Religion:

Because the Harappan script cannot be read, all reconstructions of their religion are interpretive deductions based on material iconography. We do not know the names of their gods, their prayers, or their mythologies. Always employ nuanced phrasing ("interpreted as", "suggests") in UPSC Mains answers.

10.1 The Pashupati / Proto-Shiva Seal (Mohenjo-daro)

  • Discovered by John Marshall, Seal 420 depicts a male deity seated on a low stool/dais in a yogic posture (padmasana).
  • The figure wears a three-horned conical headdress, has three faces (tricephalic), and wears armlets and bangles.
  • Surrounded by four wild animals representing the cardinal directions:
    1. Elephant (top right)
    2. Tiger (bottom right)
    3. Rhinoceros (bottom left)
    4. Water Buffalo (top left)
  • Beneath the stool, two antelopes / deer are depicted looking back toward the throne.
  • Marshall interpreted this figure as a "Proto-Shiva" exhibiting three classical aspects of later Hinduism: Pashupati (Lord of Beasts), Mahayogi (the Supreme Meditator), and Trimukha (the Three-Faced God).

10.2 Cult of Nature, Trees, Animals & Fire Installations

  • Pipal Tree Reverence: Multiple seals depict a deity standing inside the branches of a sacred pipal tree (Ficus religiosa) with a horned worshipper and a sacrificed goat below.
  • Zoolatry (Animal Worship): Mythical unicorn, humped zebu bull, and snake (naga) worship are suggested by seals and terracotta amulets.
  • Phallic & Yoni Worship: Polished stone cones (interpreted as lingas) and perforated circular ring-stones (interpreted as yonis) indicate early fertility cults.
  • Fire Altars & Ritual Ablution:
    • Kalibangan: A prominent row of seven brick-lined sacrificial fire pits was unearthed on an elevated mud-brick platform in the Citadel. Crucially, these were accompanied by associated well and paved bathing platforms, proving that fire rituals involved ceremonial water ablution / purification.
    • Banawali & Lothal: Rectangular and circular fire-altar-like installations containing charcoal, ash, and terracotta triangular cakes were discovered, indicating that ritual practices involving fire were widespread in the eastern and southern domains, distinct from the Great Bath tradition of the Indus core.

10.3 Funerary Rites & Burials

Burial Typology Specific Archaeological Evidence & Sites Socio-Cultural Interpretation (UPSC Nuance)
Extended Inhumation Dead body laid supine in an open pit, aligned strictly North to South (head towards North, feet towards South). The most common Harappan burial custom; practiced at Harappa (Cemetery R-37), Kalibangan, and Rakhigarhi.
Grave Goods Ceramic pots, copper mirrors, shell spoons, steatite beads, and toilet kits deposited inside the grave. Belief in an afterlife or soul journey; personal belongings buried with the deceased. Gold was rarely buried, showing wealth was inherited by the living.
Fractional / Secondary Burial Selected bone fragments interred in pits or jars after the deceased's body was exposed to scavenger birds and elements. Documented at Harappa and Mohenjo-daro; reflects layered, multi-stage mortuary rites across different social communities.
Double / Joint Burial A single grave pit containing the skeletons of a male and female buried together at Lothal. Debated: romanticized by early excavators as an ancient precursor to Sati, but modern forensics identifies it as simultaneous death from epidemic infectious disease.
Pot / Urn / Post-Cremation Burials Bones and ashes deposited inside large clay urns placed under stone cairns (Surkotada, Kalibangan, and Harappa). Provides material evidence consistent with cremation or post-cremation secondary interments in specific regional contexts.
Coffin Burial Dead body enclosed in a scooped-out scented rosewood coffin with a deodar-wood lid at Harappa. Foreign Mesopotamian mercantile contact or elite status.
UPSC Trap — No Universal Mortuary Custom: Do not assert that the Harappans followed one single, universal burial practice. The defining archaeological hallmark is pronounced regional and typological variation—primary inhumation, fractional burial, double burial, and urn/post-cremation interments coexisted across different settlements and phases.

11.2 Foreign Trade with Mesopotamia — Meluhha, Dilmun & Magan

  • Dilmun: Identified as the island of Bahrain and coastal eastern Arabia in the Persian Gulf. Functioned as the primary maritime clearing port and trans-shipment hub between India and Mesopotamia.
  • Magan (Makkan): Identified as the Oman Peninsula (famous for exporting copper with trace nickel impurities identical to Harappan copper).
  • Archaeological Proof:
    • Over 30 authentic Harappan steatite seals, etched carnelian beads, and cubical weights have been discovered at Mesopotamian sites including Ur, Kish, Susa, Lagash, Nippur, and Tell Asmar.
    • A circular Persian Gulf button seal was found at Lothal.
    • Cuneiform texts refer to specialized Mesopotamian royal translators: the "Dragoman of Meluhha".

12. The Decline & Transformation (c. 1900–1300 BCE)

Between 1900 and 1700 BCE, the monumental urban systems of the Harappan civilization collapsed: cities were gradually abandoned, writing disappeared, standardization ceased, and long-distance trade withered away.

12.1 Dismantling Outdated Myths — The "Aryan Invasion" Fallacy

Why the "Aryan Invasion Theory" is Rejected:

In 1947, Sir Mortimer Wheeler dramatically proclaimed that "Indra stands accused" of annihilating the Harappan cities, citing 37 unburied skeletons found in the streets of Mohenjo-daro as evidence of a genocidal massacre by Rigvedic chariot warriors. Modern archaeological analysis has completely demolished Wheeler's thesis:

  • Stratigraphic Disconnect: The skeletons belong to multiple different stratigraphic levels spanning several centuries; they do not represent a single simultaneous massacre.
  • Absence of Military Carnage: Forensic analysis shows no sword wounds or battle injuries; the skeletons reflect unburied burials or victims of epidemic disease and localized urban decay.
  • Chronological Gap: The mature urban cities declined around c. 1900 BCE, whereas the Rigvedic people appeared in north-western India around c. 1500 BCE—a 400-year hiatus!

12.2 Multi-Causal Scientific Explanations

Causal Factor Scientific Evidence & Scholars Mechanism of Collapse

Modern Academic Conclusion: Transformation, Not Extinction:

The Harappan civilisation did not vanish into thin air. What collapsed was the urban superstructure (cities, script, seals, standardized weights). The people migrated eastward and southward into the fertile Gangetic plain and Gujarat, merging into the rural Late Harappan regional cultures:

  • Cemetery H Culture: Succeeded Mature Harappa in Punjab (characterized by red ware painted with stars and flying peacocks containing human soul burials).
  • Jhukar Culture: Succeeded Mature Mohenjo-daro and Chanhudaro in Sindh.
  • Rangpur & Lustrous Red Ware Culture: Succeeded the mature phase in Gujarat.
  • Many Harappan cultural traditions (pipal reverence, yoga postures, vermilion in hair partings, binary counting, lost-wax casting) survived to become bedrock elements of later Indian culture!

Palaeoclimatological Reassessment:

A widely discussed Nature research analysis examined speleothem (cave stalagmite) oxygen-isotope records from the Himalayas and Meghalaya, confirming that the Indian subcontinent experienced a prolonged ~400-year aridity crisis between 2100 BCE and 1700 BCE. This chronic weakening of the summer monsoon stressed the rainfed and inundation agriculture around Harappan cities, triggering a gradual, voluntary migration toward smaller, water-secure settlements in the Ganga basin and Gujarat.

Dholavira utilized check-dams and 16 rock-cut reservoirs. Eliminate dogmatic statements asserting canal irrigation was either universally present or universally absent.
Animal Husbandry & Traction Humped zebu cattle (Bos indicus) and buffaloes provided agricultural traction and cart transport. Sheep, goats, pigs, and dogs were reared. Camels (bones at Kalibangan), donkeys/asses, and domestic cats were present. Evidence for the horse remains limited, debated, and atypical (e.g. Surkotada), and must never be equated with Rigvedic horse-chariot warfare. Rigvedic vs. Harappan comparative traps (UPSC 2017 Prelims); faunal diagnostic identification.
Aquatic & Marine Economy Intensive riverine and marine resource exploitation (marine catfish, molluscs, shells). Fish bones and marine shell remains at inland urban centers (e.g. Harappa) indicate that processed, salted/dried fish and coastal products were transported hundreds of kilometres inland. Evidence of inter-regional dietary transport and trade integration between coastal and inland zones.
Internal Trade & Metrology Bullock-drawn carts and pack animals linked specialized craft centers, mining zones, and farming settlements. Regulated by strictly standardised chert weights (binary lower progression, decimal higher; 16th unit = 13.64 g). Exchange operated through measured quantities, civic redistribution, and reciprocal trade rather than crude, unstructured barter. Conceptual question on state administration without minted metallic coinage.
Foreign Maritime Trade Mesopotamian cuneiform records (Sargon of Akkad, c. 2350 BCE) cite maritime trade with Meluhha (Harappa), Dilmun (Bahrain), and Magan (Oman). Harappan seals, etched carnelian beads, and weights excavated at Ur, Kish, Susa, Lagash, and Tell Asmar; circular Persian Gulf button seal found at Lothal. International commercial diplomacy and port identification in Prelims & Mains.
Coastal & Frontier Outposts Sutkagen Dor (westernmost outpost on Dasht River) and Sotka Koh (Makran coast) controlled Arabian Sea cabotage and Persian Gulf sea lanes. Balakot was a major coastal craft center specializing in marine shell working (Turbinella pyrum). Lothal operated a tidal brick basin and bead export emporium. Strategic maritime trade geography; port-site mapping in Prelims.
Art, Sculpture & Recreation Dancing Girl (Mohenjo-daro, bronze, lost-wax / cire perdue, National Museum); Priest-King (Mohenjo-daro, steatite, modern interpretive title); Red Sandstone Male Torso (Harappa, naturalistic musculature, socket holes for movable limbs); terracotta toys (bullock carts, whistle birds). Terracotta cubical dice (1–6 dots) and gaming boards prove indoor recreational board games (avoid the exaggerated claim that "gambling was their national obsession"). UPSC 2025 Prelims direct question (Dancing Girl = bronze); secular art and recreational life in Mains.
Religion & Cultic Practices Prominent row of 7 brick fire altars on Citadel platform at Kalibangan, accompanied by an associated well and paved bathing platforms for ceremonial water ablution; fire installations also at Banawali and Lothal. Pashupati seal, Mother Goddess figurines, and pipal tree veneration. Interpret cautiously because script is undeciphered. Regional cultic divergence (Indus core bathing vs. eastern/Gujarat fire ritual); Prelims 2013 trap on temples/palaces.
Mortuary Customs Primary extended inhumation (North-South; Harappa Cemetery R-37); fractional/secondary burials (Harappa, Mohenjo-daro); double/joint burial (Lothal); pot/urn burials (Surkotada, Kalibangan, Harappa); rosewood coffin burial (Harappa). No single universal burial custom existed; marked regional and typological variation. UPSC statement traps asserting uniform funeral rites; sociological diversity in Mains.
The Indus Script Undeciphered; logo-syllabic (~400–450 signs); standard writing direction is predominantly Right to Left; rare boustrophedon (alternating right-to-left and left-to-right) occurs on occasional multi-line inscriptions, but boustrophedon was NOT the normal rule. Dholavira 10-sign crystalline gypsum signboard. Epigraphical directionality and typology traps in Prelims.
Political Organisation Robust civic administration inferred indirectly from monumental public works, flood defenses, and empire-wide standardisation (1:2:4 bricks, weights, drainage). Merchant dominance is an unproven hypothesis. Leading scholarly consensus favors a decentralized network of regional city-states or municipal councils (Kenoyer) rather than a monolithic empire. Mains political governance analysis; debunking the "merchant oligarchy" assumption.

Mains GS-I 2025
"Discuss the salient features of the Harappan architecture." (150 Words | 10 Marks)
Mains Model Framework

Structured Answer Architecture

• Introduction: Define Harappan architecture as South Asia's first planned urban tradition (c. 2600–1900 BCE), characterized by functional civic design over monumental autocracy.

• Key Salient Features:
1. Spatial Layout: Dual zoning (Citadel + Lower Town); tripartite division at Dholavira; grid street layout.
2. Standardised Masonry: Uniform 1:2:4 baked bricks in English bond; dressed stone architecture at Dholavira.
3. Hydraulic & Public Works: The Great Bath (bitumen waterproofing); Dholavira's 16 rock-cut reservoirs and check-dams.
4. Domestic Design: Central courtyard, windowless outer walls for privacy, bathrooms, multi-storey staircases.
5. Subterranean Drainage: Covered street drains with removable slabs and inspection sump pits.

• Conclusion: Harappan architecture was fundamentally egalitarian, civic, and ecologically adapted, establishing planning benchmarks relevant to modern urban governance.

Mains GS-I 2014
To what extent has the urban planning and culture of the Indus Valley Civilisation provided inputs to the present-day urbanisation? (150 Words | 10 Marks)
Mains Model Framework

Structured Answer Architecture

• Introduction: Harappan urbanism was an extraordinary achievement in civic engineering that offers enduring solutions to contemporary Indian urban challenges.

• Direct Contemporary Inputs:
1. Grid Planning: Precursor to Chandigarh and modern smart cities, ensuring orderly traffic and functional zoning.
2. Drainage & Waste Management: Covered underground drains and soakage pits address contemporary urban waterlogging and open-sewer diseases.
3. Rainwater Harvesting: Dholavira's check-dams and interconnected reservoirs provide blueprints for drought-prone urban centers like Chennai and Bengaluru.
4. Material Standardization: Standardized brick ratios and weight systems reflect modern BIS building codes and municipal bylaws.
5. Climate-Responsive Housing: Courtyard layouts and natural ventilation reduce urban heat-island effects.

• Conclusion: Ancient Harappan urbanism proves that Indian cities were once global benchmarks for sanitary engineering; rediscovering these indigenous principles is essential for modern sustainable urbanisation.
