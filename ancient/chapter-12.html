1. Historical Significance — Why the Mauryas Matter

The rise of the Mauryan dynasty (c. 322/321 BCE – 185 BCE) marks a decisive watershed in the political, administrative, and cultural evolution of the Indian subcontinent. For the first time in Indian history, an unprecedented degree of territorial consolidation brought almost the entire subcontinent—spanning from modern Afghanistan and the Hindukush down to northern Karnataka and Andhra Pradesh—under a single, centralized administrative umbrella.

First Pan-Subcontinental Empire

Transformed the regional kingdom of Magadha into a vast imperial state. It eliminated the fragmented Gana-Sanghas and regional monarchies of the second urbanisation, establishing an empire that surpassed even the later British Indian empire in north-western reach (encompassing Kandahar, Kabul, and Herat).

Institutional Bureaucracy

Established an elaborate, salaried bureaucratic machine with defined hierarchies of superintendents (Adhyakshas), high dignitaries (Tirthas), district executives (Pradeshika, Rajuka, Yukta), and urban committees, superseding archaic kinship-based governance.

Epigraphic Imperial Communication

Pioneered the use of permanent, monumental stone inscriptions across major transport arteries, trade routes, and frontier zones to propagate state policy, law, and ethical governance directly to the populace in their regional vernaculars.

Ethical Statecraft Experiment

Ashoka’s formulation of Dhamma represented a historic departure from aggressive imperial militarism (Digvijaya) toward ethical-moral hegemony (Dhammavijaya) designed to maintain social equilibrium within an ethnically, linguistically, and religiously diverse empire.

2. Literary & Archaeological Sources for the Mauryas

The reconstruction of Mauryan history relies on a fortunate convergence of indigenous literature, foreign accounts, epigraphy, and material numismatics.

Source Author / Origin Key Contents & Historical Value UPSC Traps & Critical Notes
Arthashastra Kautilya (Chanakya / Vishnugupta) 15 Adhikaranas (books), 150 Prakaranas (sections), 6,000 verses. Discovered in 1905 by R. Shamasastry on palm leaves and published in 1909. Comprehensive treatise on statecraft, political economy, judicial administration, espionage, and interstate relations (Mandala theory). Trap: Not a pure contemporary historical narrative; it is a normative/prescriptive theoretical manual. Parts were augmented up to the early centuries CE, though its core reflects early Mauryan statecraft.
Indica Megasthenes (Seleucid Ambassador) Detailed eyewitness observations of Chandragupta Maurya’s court at Pataliputra, which Megasthenes renders in Greek as Palibothra. Records municipal administration (6 boards), military war office (6 boards), and social divisions. Trap: The original book is completely lost! It survives only as quoted fragments in later Classical writers (Strabo, Arrian, Diodorus Siculus, Pliny). Recorded incorrect claims (e.g., Indians did not know writing, no slavery, 7 castes).
Mudrarakshasa Vishakhadatta Sanskrit historical political drama composed during the Gupta period (c. 5th–6th century CE). Dramatizes the intricate political intrigues and stratagems of Chanakya / Kautilya to overthrow the Nandas, outwit Dhana Nanda’s loyal minister Rakshasa, and firmly establish Chandragupta Maurya on the throne. Trap: Composed centuries after the Mauryas; reflects Gupta-era literary flair, though it preserves genuine historical traditions of Mauryan court intrigue.
Ceylonese Chronicles Dipavamsa & Mahavamsa Pali monastic chronicles of Sri Lanka. Provide chronological frameworks for Ashoka’s reign, his conversion to Buddhism, the Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra, and the dispatch of Mahendra and Sanghamitra to Ceylon. Trap: Heavily theologized; portrays Ashoka as an excessively wicked tyrant (Chandashoka) before his miraculous transformation into pious Dharmashoka to glorify Buddhism.
Jaina Traditions Parishishtaparvan (Hemachandra) & Bhadrabahu-charita Details Chandragupta Maurya’s royal patronage of Jainism, a 12-year famine in Magadha, his abdication, and migration to Shravanabelagola in Karnataka with Acharya Bhadrabahu. Caution: This association is mainly preserved through later Jain traditions rather than contemporary Mauryan inscriptions.
Sohgaura Copper Plate Gorakhpur District, Uttar Pradesh Archaic Brahmi inscription on a small copper plate, traditionally associated with the Mauryan era. Contains line drawings of three-storeyed granaries (Koshthagara) and explicitly refers to grain-storage arrangements and state famine-relief measures. High-Yield Prelims Point: Earliest known epigraphic evidence in India specifically documenting state grain storage and emergency famine administration.
Mahasthan Inscription Bogra District, Bangladesh Early fragmentary Brahmi inscription (in Prakrit) recording relief measures during a period of scarcity/famine in ancient Pundranagara. Mentions the distribution of paddy and loans (Gandaka coins). Confirms Mauryan-era state disaster-management and administrative reach into the Bengal delta.
Epigraphic Edicts (Dhamma-lipi) Emperor Ashoka First decipherable epigraphs in Indian history, deciphered in 1837 by James Prinsep (Civil Servant and Secretary of the Asiatic Society of Bengal). Primary contemporary evidence for Ashoka's policies, Dhamma, and empire boundaries. Ashoka calls his own inscriptions Dhamma-lipi ("writings concerning Dhamma"). 2016 PYQ: James Prinsep deciphered Ashoka’s Brahmi script in 1837 (not 1838). Edicts provide unvarnished imperial voice, containing administrative, political, and ethical directives, not merely religious scriptures.
Junagadh Rock Inscription Mahakshatrapa Rudradaman I (150 CE) Written in chaste Sanskrit. Explicitly mentions that the Sudarsana Lake dam was originally constructed by Chandragupta Maurya’s provincial governor (Rashtriya) Pushyagupta, and canals were subsequently excavated by Ashoka’s governor Tushaspha (a Yavana). Trap: The inscription is centuries later than the Mauryas; it preserves historical data about earlier Mauryan hydraulic engineering.

3. Chandragupta Maurya (c. 322/321 – 297 BCE)

Chandragupta Maurya was the architect of the Mauryan Empire. Guided by Kautilya (Chanakya), he capitalized on widespread popular disaffection against the corrupt Nanda dynasty—specifically overthrowing the last Nanda ruler, Dhana Nanda—and the geopolitical vacuum left in north-western India following Alexander’s departure and death in 323 BCE.

UPSC Nuance & Correction — Extent of Conquest

Do NOT write that Chandragupta “conquered the whole of Aryavarta” as a precise historical fact. His territorial expansion was extensive, especially across northern and north-western India, but “entire Aryavarta” is too sweeping and uncritical for UPSC answers.

The Seleucid–Mauryan Conflict & Settlement (c. 305–303 BCE)

  • Conflict: Occurred c. 305–303 BCE when Seleucus I Nicator crossed the Indus to reclaim Alexander’s eastern conquests.
  • Territorial Settlement in the North-West: Seleucus ceded extensive north-western territories to Chandragupta, including regions corresponding broadly to parts of modern Afghanistan and Baluchistan: Aria (Herat), Arachosia (Kandahar), Gedrosia (Baluchistan), and Paropamisadae (Kabul).
  • War Elephants: Chandragupta gave Seleucus 500 war elephants in return, which proved decisive in Seleucus' victory at the Battle of Ipsus (301 BCE) in Phrygia.
  • Epigamia / Marriage Alliance Nuance: Greek texts mention a marital/diplomatic arrangement (Epigamia). The precise nature of this alliance is historically debated among scholars; UPSC notes should not present a dramatic “Seleucus’s daughter married Chandragupta” narrative as an unquestionable historical fact.
  • Diplomatic Envoy: Megasthenes was subsequently dispatched as the permanent Seleucid ambassador to the Mauryan imperial court at Pataliputra (Palibothra).
UPSC Trap — Territorial Reality

Seleucus did not simply “lose Afghanistan”; rather, a formal political and territorial settlement in the north-west followed the conflict, securing the Mauryan scientific frontier along the Hindukush.

Chandragupta, Jainism & Shravanabelagola

  • According to Jaina tradition (preserved in Hemachandra’s Parishishtaparvan and the Bhadrabahu-charita), a severe 12-year famine struck Magadha toward the end of Chandragupta's reign.
  • Chandragupta abdicated the throne in favor of his son Bindusara, converted to Jainism, and accompanied the revered Jain teacher Bhadrabahu to the south.
  • He settled at Shravanabelagola (Hassan District, Karnataka) and ended his life by voluntary ritual starvation unto death (Sallekhana / Santhara) on the Chandragiri hill.
  • Historiographical Note: This tradition is preserved primarily in later Jain literary and epigraphic sources (e.g., 7th–10th century CE Karnataka inscriptions) rather than contemporary Mauryan epigraphs.

4. Bindusara (c. 297 – 273 BCE) — Amitraghata

Chandragupta was succeeded by his son Bindusara. He maintained the territorial integrity of the empire and preserved active diplomatic links with contemporary Hellenistic realms.

Amitraghata / Amitrochates

Greek sources preserve Bindusara's name as Amitrochates or Allitrochades, correctly linked with the Sanskrit epithet Amitraghata ("Slayer / Destroyer of Foes"). In the Puranas, he is also called Bhadrasara.

Antiochus I & The Sophist Anecdote

A famous classical tradition records that Bindusara wrote to the Seleucid monarch Antiochus I requesting sweet wine, dried figs, and a Greek sophist/philosopher. Antiochus dispatched the wine and figs, but replied that Greek law strictly forbade the selling/exporting of a philosopher.

Territorial Expansion Nuance

Later literary accounts (e.g., Tibetan historian Taranatha) attribute to Bindusara conquest of the territory "between the eastern and western seas" (peninsular India). This must be treated cautiously, as contemporary inscriptional evidence is absent.

Association with the Ajivikas

Later traditions associate Bindusara with the Ajivika sect. Rather than stating categorically that he "joined" the Ajivikas, write that he supported and patronised the Ajivikas (e.g., the Ajivika fortune-teller Pingalavatsa / Janasana at his court).

Taxila Pacification: During his reign, citizens of Taxila revolted against the oppression of local wicked ministers (Dushamatyas). Bindusara dispatched Prince Ashoka, who peacefully pacified the province without military violence.

5. Ashoka the Great (c. 268 – 232 BCE) — Accession & Titles

Prior to his accession, Ashoka served as provincial viceroy at both Ujjain (Avantiratha) and Taxila (Uttarapatha), gaining extensive administrative experience on sensitive frontiers.

The Four-Year Interregnum (c. 272 – 268 BCE)

  • Bindusara passed away around 272 BCE, but Ashoka’s formal coronation (Abhisheka) occurred only in 268 BCE.
  • This four-year hiatus reflects a disputed succession. Buddhist chronicles (Mahavamsa) claim Ashoka killed 99 half-brothers, sparing only Tissa, with the assistance of minister Radhagupta.
  • Historical Reality: In Major Rock Edict V, Ashoka explicitly mentions the households and welfare of his brothers and sisters living peacefully in Pataliputra and outlying provincial towns, proving the 99-slaughter tale is pious monastic exaggeration.

Royal Inscriptional Titles

  • Devanampriya / Devanampiya: "Beloved of the Gods".
  • Priyadarshi / Piyadasi: "He Who Looks with Affection" or "Of Pleasant Appearance".
High-Yield Prelims Focus — The Four Personal Name Sites

Only four inscriptions mention the monarch explicitly by his personal name Ashoka:
1. Maski (Raichur district, Karnataka) — First discovered in 1915.
2. Gujjarra (Datia district, Madhya Pradesh) — Reads Devanampiya Piyadassi Asokaraja.
3. Nittur (Bellary district, Karnataka).
4. Udegolam (Bellary district, Karnataka).

2019 PYQ: A stone relief slab showing Ashoka with queens inscribed with "Ranyo Ashoka" (King Ashoka) was discovered at Kanaganahalli (Sannati, Bhima river, Kalaburagi/Gulbarga district, Karnataka).

6. The Kalinga War (c. 261 BCE) & Bherighosha to Dhammaghosha

The decisive turning point of Ashoka’s reign occurred in his 8th regnal year (c. 261 BCE)—correctly dated around 261 BCE, NOT 269 BCE.

Why Kalinga Mattered

Strategic Coastal Geography

Kalinga occupied a critical strategic coastal position on the Bay of Bengal, controlling vital overland and maritime communication corridors connecting the Gangetic plains with South India, Sri Lanka, and Southeast Asia.

Economic & Artisanal Strength

Renowned for active maritime trade, ports, skilled craft production, and war elephants, Kalinga was a prosperous independent power right on Magadha’s maritime flank. Fortified urban centres like Sisupalgarh (near Bhubaneswar) reveal planned layouts, iron implements, NBPW, and punch-marked coins from this era.

Bherighosha to Dhammaghosha (Major Rock Edicts IV & XIII)

Bherighosha: The sound of the war drum signifying imperial military conquest.
Dhammaghosha: The sound of righteousness proclaiming the ethical principles of Dhamma.
In Major Rock Edict IV, Ashoka explicitly contrasts past centuries of military violence with the moral transformation of society, declaring that the sound of the war drum (Bherighosha) has become the sound of Dhamma (Dhammaghosha). In Major Rock Edict XIII, he describes his intense remorse after the slaughter of 100,000 and capture of 150,000 individuals in Kalinga, formally renouncing aggressive conquest (Digvijaya) in favor of moral victory (Dhammavijaya).

UPSC Trap — Ashoka's Pacifism vs. Imperial Statecraft

Did Ashoka disband the Mauryan army or abolish capital punishment after Kalinga? NO!
1. The standing army was never disbanded; imperial military power remained intact.
2. In Major Rock Edict XIII, Ashoka issued a stern warning to the forest tribes (Atavikas): the King forgives what can be forgiven, but reminds them of his power to punish them if they continue predatory acts.
3. Capital punishment was not abolished. Pillar Edict IV merely granted a three-day stay of execution for condemned prisoners to settle their affairs or seek spiritual merit.

7. Why Did the Mauryans Require a Complex Administration?

Forests & Tribal Integration

Vast forested tracts (Atavika zones) inhabited by independent hunter-gatherer and tribal communities had to be brought into the imperial geopolitical framework without provoking perpetual rebellion.

Surplus Extraction & Records

The expanding agrarian base required elaborate machinery to systematically survey lands, assess crops, collect revenues in cash and kind, and maintain meticulous district audit registers.

Regulation of Urban Centres

Flourishing craft production and long-distance trade necessitated close state supervision of artisan guilds, standardized weights and measures, market prices, toll collection, and crime prevention.

Subcontinental Imperial Scale

Governing distant provinces spanning from the Afghan border to the Deccan required rapid communication networks, viceregal councils, standing garrisons, and extensive surveillance systems.

8. Central Administration & Arthashastra Kingship Ideals

The central administration of the Mauryas operated under seven functional pillars: The King, Council of Ministers, City Administration, Army Administration, Espionage Network, Law & Justice, and Public Welfare.

Kautilya's Saptanga Theory (Seven Limbs of the State)

  1. Swami: The King / Sovereign (the head).
  2. Amatya: Ministers and high officials (the eyes).
  3. Janapada: Territory and population (the legs/thighs).
  4. Durga: Fortified capital (the arms).
  5. Kosha: Treasury (the mouth).
  6. Danda (Bala): Army (the brain).
  7. Mitra: Allies (the ears).

Arthashastra: Normative Qualities Expected of the King

  • Uchchakula: Noble and esteemed birth.
  • Dharmapravartaka: Active promoter and upholder of Dharma.
  • Subordinate Control: Firm capacity to keep feudatories and officials strictly under imperial discipline.
  • Sharp Intellect & Truthfulness: Keen foresight, training in sciences, and unyielding adherence to truth.
UPSC Critical Distinction

These are normative ideals of kingship laid down in the Arthashastra; they should not automatically be taken as empirical evidence that every historical Mauryan monarch possessed all these virtues!

9. Council of Ministers (Mantriparishad) & The 18 Tirthas

Kautilya famously emphasized: "Sovereignty is possible only with assistance. A single wheel can never turn."

Integrity & Decision-Making

  • Ministers were rigorously tested through secret integrity tests (Upadhas) testing their resistance to religious fear, financial greed, lust, and physical peril.
  • The highest standard required ministers to be Sarvopashuddha — "pure and unblemished in all respects".
  • Collective Advice: Kautilya recommends consulting the council and considers majority advice desirable in crises, though the King retained final political and legislative authority.

11. Provincial, District & Local Administration (2025 PYQ)

The Five Imperial Provinces (Chakras)

Province (Chakra) Provincial Capital Geopolitical Sphere
Uttarapatha Taxila North-western frontier, Gandhara, and Afghan trade corridors.
Avantirashtra Ujjain Western province, Malwa, and ports of Gujarat.
Prachi (Prachya) Pataliputra (Palibothra) Eastern heartland, Magadha, and lower Gangetic basin.
Kalinga Tosali (Dhauli) Conquered eastern coastal province.
Dakshinapatha Suvarnagiri (Karnataka) Southern peninsular province and gold-mining zone.

Provinces were governed by royal princes styled Kumaras or Aryaputras, assisted by councils of Mahamatras.

2025 UPSC Prelims Focus — The Three-Tier Administrative Chain

• Provincial Tier: Kumaras / Aryaputras assisted by Mahamatras.
• District Tier (Ahara / Vishaya):
  1. Pradeshika: District Executive Head; law and order, general supervision, 5-year inspection tours.
  2. Rajuka: Revenue survey and judicial magistrate (held the measuring rope/rajju); given independent judicial authority by Ashoka (PE IV).
  3. Yukta: Subordinate accounts and secretarial officer managing district documentation.
• Local / Village Tier:
  1. Gramika: Village headman elected/appointed by village elders (Gramavriddhas).
  2. Gopa: Intermediate registrar overseeing 5 to 10 villages (census, boundaries, land).
  3. Sthanika: District tax official heading one-fourth of a district, supervising the Gopas.

12. Municipal Administration: Megasthenes' Six Committees

Crucial Historiographical Distinction

The system of six city committees of five members each (30 commissioners total) is specifically recorded by Megasthenes for Pataliputra (Palibothra). The Arthashastra describes corresponding civic functions managed by individual superintendents (Adhyakshas) rather than this exact 6-board committee system.

Committee Portfolio Name Specific Civic & Regulatory Duties
1st Board Industrial Arts Supervision of craft production, wages, materials, and artisan protection.
2nd Board Foreigners Lodging, surveillance, medical care, and burial arrangements for foreign visitors.
3rd Board Births & Deaths Vital statistics registration for tax assessment, law enforcement, and census.
4th Board Trade & Commerce Market regulation, inspection of weights and measures, commodity licensing.
5th Board Manufactured Goods Prevention of commercial fraud; prohibiting mixing of new goods with old/used goods.
6th Board Tithe / Sales Tax Collection of a 10% sales tax (tenth) on all goods sold in the marketplace.

Key Municipal Officials in the Arthashastra

  • Nagarika: Chief City Governor / Administrator.
  • Sulkadhyaksha: Collector of customs, entry tolls, and gate dues.
  • Bandhanagaradhyaksha: Superintendent of city prisons.
  • Rakshi: City police guards maintaining night curfews.

13. Army Administration: Megasthenes vs. Arthashastra

UPSC Trap — Do Not Mix the Two Military Systems

1. Megasthenes' Military War Office: 30 members divided into six committees of five each: (1) Infantry, (2) Cavalry, (3) War Elephants, (4) War Chariots, (5) Transport & Commissariat, and (6) Admiralty / Navy.
2. Arthashastra's Traditional Chaturangabala: Standard fourfold fighting arms: (1) Infantry (Patti), (2) Cavalry (Ashva), (3) Chariots (Ratha), and (4) Elephants (Hasti).

Key Military Officials: Senapati (Supreme Commander), Nayaka (Operational Field Commander), Ayudhyagaradhyaksha (Armory Superintendent), Rathadhyaksha (Chariot Corps), and Hastyadhyaksha (Elephant Corps). The Mauryan military was a permanent, professional standing army paid regular salaries in cash directly from the state treasury.

14. Judiciary & The Four Sources of Law

The King was the supreme fountain of justice and court of final appeal. Below the monarch operated two distinct categories of courts:

Dharmasthiya Courts (Civil)

Presided over by three Dharmasthas and three Amatyas. Adjudicated private legal disputes: contracts, recovery of debts, boundary disputes, inheritance, marriage, and defamation.

Kantakashodhana Courts (Criminal)

"Suppression of Thorns". Special courts headed by three Pradeshtris. Dealt with crimes against the state: treason, official extortion, merchant hoarding, adulteration, theft, murder, and anti-state conspiracies.

The Four Sources of Law in the Arthashastra

  1. Dharma: Sacred moral law and eternal truth.
  2. Vyavahara: Judicial procedure, evidence, contracts, and witness testimony.
  3. Charita (Charitra): Established customs, traditions, and community usage.
  4. Rajashasana: Royal edicts and sovereign decrees (held supreme in case of conflict).
UPSC Nuance — Uniformity of Law (Pillar Edict IV)

In Major Pillar Edict IV, Ashoka proclaimed his desire for Vyavahara-samata (uniformity in judicial procedure) and Danda-samata (uniformity in penalties), while investing the Rajukas with wide judicial discretion over rewards and punishments.

Exam Trap: Do NOT claim that Ashoka established a modern egalitarian legal code where everyone was equal before the law. In actual practice, varna distinctions and social status continued to influence legal penalties.

16. Revenue Administration: 7 Sources & Key Tax Terms

The Sannidhata was the chief custodian of the state treasury and storehouses, while the Samaharta assessed and collected revenue across seven primary sources.

The Seven Revenue Heads in the Arthashastra

Head Sanskrit Term Resource Base & Scope
1 Durga Urban centres and fortified towns (customs, sales tax, court fines, excise).
2 Rashtra Rural countryside and agricultural territories (land revenues, grain levies).
3 Khani Mines, quarries, and metallurgical extraction.
4 Setu Irrigated plantations, fruit orchards, flower gardens, and sugarcane fields.
5 Vana Forest produce, timber, and elephant reserves.
6 Vraja Pastures, herds, cattle, dairy produce, and horse breeding.
7 Vanikpatha Trade routes, river highways, road tolls, and transport dues.

Key Fiscal & Tax Terminology

  • Bhaga: Principal land-revenue share. The standard normative rate was 1/6th of produce (NOT 1/4th, nor 1/2!). Reduced to 1/8th (Atthabhagiya) at Lumbini.
  • Bali: Religious or customary tribute; completely exempted (Ubalike kate) at Lumbini in the Rummindei Pillar Edict.
  • Hiranya: Revenue tax paid strictly in cash/money.
  • Pranaya: Extraordinary emergency tax levied during fiscal crises.
  • Pindakara: Lump-sum assessment levied collectively on groups of villages.
  • Senabhakta: Levy of food provisions extracted from villages for troops on the march.
  • Parsvam: Additional surcharge or margin collected above regular assessment.
  • Udakabhaga: Water / irrigation tax (ranging from 1/5th to 1/3rd).
  • Vishti: Forced, unpaid corvée labor extracted from artisans and laborers.

17. Economy, Agriculture & Sita Lands

Sita Lands (Crown Agriculture)

Vast tracts of fertile crown land directly managed by the state under the Sitadhyaksha. Cultivated by state slaves (Dasa), penal convicts, and landless sharecroppers (Ardhasitikas).

Colonisation of Virgin Land

The state actively promoted the settlement of new/virgin territories by shifting surplus Shudra population and granting tax holidays, seeds, and bullocks.

Rural Landed Elites

Literary sources reveal substantial private landholders and rural elites styled Gahapatis and Gramabhojakas who employed hired laborers (Bhatakas).

Sharecropping Contracts

The Arthashastra provides flexible sharecropping arrangements: sharecroppers bringing their own seeds and cattle retained 1/2 of the crop; those supplied with state inputs retained 1/4th or 1/5th.

Archaeological Reality — Ring Wells

Do NOT write that "ring wells were invented by the Mauryan Empire." Terracotta ring wells were already known in the early historic urban tradition (Middle/Late NBPW phase) and became widespread during this period for domestic drainage and soak-pits, but they were not an exclusive Mauryan invention.

18. Trade Routes & Commercial Officers

Imperial pacification and road construction stimulated vibrant internal and trans-regional trade.

The Three Primary Imperial Trade Routes

Route Geographic Alignment & Commercial Role
Uttarapatha Northern highway linking Kapilavastu, Shravasti, Vaishali → Kalsi → Taxila → Pushkalavati/Peshawar and westward to Central Asia.
Eastern Route From Pataliputra through Champa towards Kalinga and down into the eastern coastal Deccan.
Dakshinapatha Southern highway from Ganga-Yamuna through Ujjain, crossing the Narmada into the Deccan and peninsular mining hubs.

Commercial Officers: Panyadhyaksha (prices & state goods), Samsthadhyaksha (market policing), Pautavadhyaksha (weights & measures), Sulkadhyaksha (customs/tolls), Navadhyaksha (river ferries & shipping), Akaradhyaksha (mines), and Lohadhyaksha (iron manufacturing).

19. Coins & Monetisation

  • The Mauryan economy was highly monetised. The standard currency was the silver punch-marked Pana (weighing approx. 3/4th tola), punched with five imperial symbols (peacock, crescent-on-hill, tree, wheel, animal).
  • Copper Punch-Marked Coins: Copper punch-marked coins (such as Mashakas and fractional Kakanis) also circulated extensively. Exam Trap: The statement that "Mauryan coins were only made of silver" is completely incorrect!
  • No Standard Gold Currency: While terms for gold are mentioned in the Arthashastra, regular imperial currency was predominantly based on silver and copper punch-marked coins; standard gold coins were issued later by the Kushans and Guptas.
  • The state maintained a strict monopoly over minting under the Lakshanadhyaksha, while the Rupa-darshaka acted as currency examiner testing alloy purity.
  • Salaries of civil and military officials were paid in cash (ranging from 48,000 panas for Mantrins to 60 panas for ordinary laborers).

20. Society, Megasthenes' 7 Castes & Slavery Laws (2022 PYQ)

Megasthenes' Seven Social Classes (Symmoriai)

Megasthenes confused occupational craft groups with hereditary varna, listing seven classes: (1) Philosophers (Brahmanas & Shramanas), (2) Cultivators / Farmers (largest class, exempt from military service), (3) Herdsmen & Hunters, (4) Artisans & Traders, (5) Warriors / Soldiers (second largest class), (6) Overseers / Inspectors (spies), and (7) Councillors & Assessors (ministers & magistrates).

UPSC Trap — Seven Classes ≠ Four Varnas

This sevenfold scheme was Megasthenes’ personal observation, not the indigenous four-varna system. The Arthashastra shows that Brahmanical ideals enjoyed high prestige, but actual recruitment (even into the army) cut across varna boundaries.

2022 UPSC Prelims Focus — Kautilya's Arthashastra on Slavery

Megasthenes wrote that "all Indians are free and slavery does not exist in India." Why was he mistaken?
Because Megasthenes compared India with brutal Greek chattel slavery where slaves were property without rights. In contrast, the Arthashastra recognizes nine categories of slaves (Dasa) and provides strict legal protections:

• An Arya could never be permanently enslaved; mortgaging an Arya minor was severely punished.
• Slaves could own and inherit property and earn independent wages in their spare time.
• A slave could purchase freedom by paying the ransom amount (Nishkraya).
• 2022 PYQ: If a female slave bore a son to her master, both the mother and the child were legally freed immediately! The child also enjoyed inheritance rights.

21. Status of Women & Marriage Institutions

  • Varied Social Position: Avoid blanket statements that women had uniformly "high" or "low" status. Greek accounts record armed female bodyguards protecting the King’s palace.
  • Stridhana: The Arthashastra explicitly recognizes Stridhana (woman’s separate property comprising wedding gifts, jewelry, and cash endowments), over which women retained significant legal control.
  • Eight Forms of Marriage: Recognized traditional marriage forms: Brahma, Daiva, Arsha, Prajapatya, Gandharva, Asura, Rakshasa, Paishacha.
  • Divorce & Remarriage: Under specific conditions of mutual enmity (Moksha) or prolonged abandonment, divorce/separation was legally sanctioned. Widow remarriage was legally recognized. Absolute claims like "child marriage was completely absent" should be avoided.

22. Public Welfare & Inscriptional Famine Relief

Medical Care (Major Rock Edict II)

Established dual dispensaries—one for humans (Manusha-chikitsa) and one for animals (Pashu-chikitsa)—extending across his empire and into neighboring Tamil kingdoms (Cholas, Pandyas, Keralaputras).

Highways (Pillar Edict VII)

Planted banyan trees and mango groves, dug wells at every half-kos (approx. 1 mile), and erected rest-houses (Saray) and watering sheds (Prapas).

Sohgaura Copper Plate (UP)

Mentions two three-storeyed granaries (Koshthagara) established to store grain and provide emergency famine relief to the populace.

Mahasthan Inscription (Bengal)

Records the distribution of emergency grain and financial loans from the city granary of Pundranagara during a period of scarcity.

Sudarsana Lake Dam: Originally constructed under Chandragupta Maurya by Pushyagupta; conduits and canals added under Ashoka by Tushaspha; repaired later by Rudradaman I (150 CE) and Skandagupta (456 CE).

23. Ashoka's Dhamma: Nature, Broad Purpose & Dhamma-Lipi

CRITICAL CORRECTION — Nature of the Rock Edicts & Dhamma-lipi

1. The 14 Major Rock Edicts are NOT simply "14 principles of Dhamma." They are fourteen numbered edicts dealing with diverse administrative, ethical, social, foreign, and political matters. Together, they articulate Ashoka’s comprehensive Dhamma policy.
2. Inscriptions are termed in the texts as Dhamma-lipi ("records concerning Dhamma"). Do not state that they were purely religious scriptures—they contain vital civic and administrative decrees.

Ashoka’s Dhamma was not a sectarian religion or monastic dogma, but an imperial integrative ethic designed to foster social cohesion, reduce communal friction, and promote moral responsibility in a vast multi-cultural empire.

Kandahar Bilingual Inscription — Hunters & Fishermen

The Kandahar Greek-Aramaic bilingual inscription in southern Afghanistan provides concrete evidence of the practical social impact of Ashoka's animal welfare policy: it explicitly records that the King had abstained from killing living beings and that hunters and fishermen associated with the royal court had given up hunting and killing.
Trap: This specific reference to hunters and fishermen is associated with the Kandahar inscription, NOT the Kalinga Edicts!

GS-I Mains Conceptual Dimension

Dhamma was an ideological tool of imperial integration. By advocating non-violence (Ahimsa), religious tolerance (Samavaya), respect for elders (Shushrusa), and humane treatment of slaves (Dasa-Bhataka), Ashoka sought to minimize social conflict without having to rely perpetually on coercive military force.

24. The Complete Set of 14 Major Rock Edicts (MRE I–XIV)

Edict Core Subject & Administrative / Ethical Decree
Major Rock Edict I Prohibits animal sacrifices in the capital; bans festive gatherings (Samajas) involving revelry; restricts animal killing in royal kitchens to two peacocks and one deer.
Major Rock Edict II Medical treatment facilities for humans and animals; planting of medicinal herbs, roots, and roadside trees; mentions southern border neighbors: Cholas, Pandyas, Satyaputras, Keralaputras, and Tamraparni (Sri Lanka).
Major Rock Edict III Orders district officers—Pradeshika, Rajuka, and Yukta—to embark on administrative inspection tours every five years; preaches obedience to parents, generosity to Brahmanas and Shramanas, and moderation in saving and spending.
Major Rock Edict IV Contrasts past centuries of violence with the present moral transformation; declares that the sound of the war drum (Bherighosha) has become the sound of righteousness (Dhammaghosha).
Major Rock Edict V Records the appointment of Dhamma-Mahamatras in his 14th regnal year; commands them to look after all sects (Brahmanas, Ajivikas, Jains, Sangha) and prisoners.
Major Rock Edict VI Orders that the King be informed of the people’s affairs at all times and in all places (inner chambers, dining hall, riding grounds) through Prativedakas (reporters).
Major Rock Edict VII Proclaims broad religious tolerance and mutual coexistence; desires that all sects may reside everywhere in peace.
Major Rock Edict VIII Replaces royal pleasure tours (Vihara-yatras) with moral pilgrimages (Dhamma-yatras); records his visit to the sacred Bodhi tree at Bodh Gaya.
Major Rock Edict IX Criticizes superstitious, expensive, and unproductive rituals performed by women during illness, weddings, or childbirth; declares the ceremony of Dhamma (courtesy to slaves, respect to elders) as far superior.
Major Rock Edict X Denounces the vain pursuit of personal fame and glory; asserts true glory lies solely in causing people to practice Dhamma.
Major Rock Edict XI Defines the highest gift as the gift of Dhamma (Dhamma-dana), consisting of liberality, non-violence, and proper social relations.
Major Rock Edict XII (2020 PYQ) The definitive charter of religious toleration: urges that one should not honor only one's own sect while condemning others; proclaims that concord (Samavaya) alone is supreme.
Major Rock Edict XIII The Kalinga War tragedy (100,000 dead, 150,000 captives); spiritual remorse; replacement of Digvijaya with Dhammavijaya; mentions five contemporary Greek monarchs and warns forest tribes (Atavikas).
Major Rock Edict XIV Explains the composition and purpose of the inscriptions—some written concisely, some moderately, and some extensively to inspire people to righteous conduct.

Statements claiming that "Dhauli and Jaugada contain the complete regular set of all 14 Major Rock Edicts" are false! At both sites, Major Rock Edicts XI, XII, and XIII are omitted and replaced by the Separate Kalinga Edicts declaring paternal royal love ("All men are my children").

26. The 7 Major Pillar Edicts & Key Pillar Sites

The Seven Major Pillar Edicts form a distinct category from the Rock Edicts. Quarried from Chunar sandstone, they were erected exclusively in the fertile Gangetic heartland.

Pillar Edict Core Theme & Administrative Provision
Pillar Edict I Principles of righteous imperial administration, protection, and welfare of subjects.
Pillar Edict II Defines Dhamma: "Apasinave, bahukayane, daya, dane, sache, sochaye" (Few sins, abundant good deeds, compassion, charity, truthfulness, purity).
Pillar Edict III Warns against moral decay and sinful passions: cruelty, anger, pride, and jealousy (Asinava).
Pillar Edict IV Governs the responsibilities of the Rajukas (invested with independent judicial discretion, seeking Vyavahara-samata and Danda-samata); grants a 3-day stay of execution for death-row inmates.
Pillar Edict V Lists protected animals, birds, and aquatic life; prohibits castration of animals on holy days; records periodic releases of prisoners.
Pillar Edict VI States the King's continuing purpose in issuing edicts for the welfare and happiness of the people.
Pillar Edict VII Comprehensive master summary of Ashoka’s Dhamma works; found EXCLUSIVELY on the Delhi-Topra pillar!

Key Pillar Locations

  • Delhi-Topra: Originally brought from Topra in Haryana to Delhi by Sultan Firoz Shah Tughlaq in the 14th century. Only pillar carrying all 7 Pillar Edicts.
  • Delhi-Meerut: Originally from Meerut (UP); transported to Delhi by Firoz Shah Tughlaq.
  • Allahabad / Prayagraj Pillar: Contains epigraphs of three historical eras: Ashoka (Pillar Edicts & Schism Edict), Samudragupta (Prayaga Prashasti by Harishena), and Mughal Emperor Jahangir.
  • Rampurva (Bihar): Known for two pillars; crowned by the famous Rampurva Bull (now at Rashtrapati Bhavan) and a single Lion capital.
  • Lauriya-Nandangarh (Bihar): Complete intact pillar crowned by a single majestic lion.
  • Lauriya-Araraj (Bihar): Monolithic shaft inscribed with Pillar Edicts I–VI.

27. Mauryan Art & Architecture: Court vs. Folk Art

Ashokan Pillars vs. Achaemenid (Persian) Pillars

Feature Mauryan / Ashokan Pillars Achaemenid Persian Pillars
Shaft Construction Monolithic; carved from a single block of Chunar sandstone with a smooth, mirror-like polish. Composed of multiple segmented stone drums fastened together with lead dowels; often fluted.
Architectural Function Free-standing monumental memorials erected independently in the open air to display royal edicts. Integrated structural members supporting massive palace ceilings, porticoes, and hypostyle halls.
Capital Structure Inverted bell-shaped lotus capital surmounted by an abacus with high-relief animal figures and a crowning sculpture. Bracket-type capitals with addorsed animal protomes (bulls/unicorns) supporting architectural roof beams.
Base Support Rise smoothly directly from the earth without any separate base plinth. Rest upon elaborately carved stone plinths or bell-shaped molded pedestals.
GS-I Mains Insight — Persian Influence Nuance

Mauryan art shows unmistakable foreign/Achaemenid exposure but not simple imitation. Indian master craftsmen creatively adapted external lapidary polishing techniques into an original, free-standing monumental idiom.

Stupa Architectural Anatomy & The Torana Trap

Architectural Element Structural & Symbolic Function
Anda The solid hemispherical earthen/brick dome forming the monumental core, representing the cosmic dome.
Harmika A square railed balcony atop the Anda, symbolizing the sacred dwelling place of the gods and enshrining the relic.
Chatra (Chhatra) The central triple-tiered umbrella rising from the harmika, symbolizing universal sovereignty, protection, and the Three Jewels (Triratna).
Torana Ornamental ceremonial gateways set at the four cardinal directions, decorated with narrative relief panels.
UPSC Trap — Sanchi Toranas Were NOT Built by Ashoka

Ashoka is traditionally credited with building the early unburnt brick core of Stupa 1 at Sanchi. However, the famous, elaborately carved stone Toranas (gateways) were NOT constructed by Ashoka—they were added centuries later during the Satavahana period (c. 1st century BCE)! The outer stone encasing and railings were largely added during the Shunga-Satavahana periods.

Rock-Cut Cave Architecture & The Lomas Rishi Facade

  • Barabar Caves (Jehanabad, Bihar): Excavated under Ashoka and dedicated specifically to the AJIVIKAS (Sudama, Lomas Rishi, Karan Chaupar, Visva Zopri). Characterized by simple interior plans and mirror-like polished granite walls.
  • Nagarjuni Caves: Dedicated by Ashoka's grandson Dasharatha Maurya to the Ajivikas.
  • Lomas Rishi Cave Significance: Features an ornamental entrance facade meticulously imitating a wooden barrel-vaulted hut with a chaitya arch and carved elephants. This represents decisive archaeological proof of the transition from wooden architectural traditions to permanent stone/rock-cut architecture in India.
  • Vajrasana (Diamond Throne): Polished stone slab at Bodh Gaya beneath the Bodhi tree marking the seat of the Buddha's enlightenment, traditionally linked with Ashokan patronage.
  • Dhauli Rock-Cut Elephant: Carved out of living rock directly above the Dhauli edict face (Odisha), representing the earliest rock-cut animal sculpture in India.
  • Kumrahar Pillared Hall: Massive timber palisades and 84-column polished sandstone hypostyle hall at Pataliputra, demonstrating royal wooden architecture.

Popular & Folk Art: Yakshas, Yakshinis & Terracotta

  • Didarganj Yakshi / Chauri Bearer: Famous life-sized polished sandstone sculpture found at Didarganj near Patna holding a flywhisk. While traditionally attributed to the Mauryan period due to its lustrous Mauryan polish, its exact chronological dating remains debated among scholars (some assign it to later Kushan or post-Mauryan periods).
  • Parkham & Besnagar Figures: Massive sandstone Yaksha (Mathura) and Yakshi (Vidisha) statues symbolizing folk fertility and guardian deities.
  • Terracotta Figurines: Widespread popular figurines depicting mother-goddess cults, dancers, and animals (elephants, rams), illustrating that Mauryan artistic culture encompassed both elite court monuments and popular folk traditions.

28. The Third Buddhist Council at Pataliputra

  • Date & Venue: Convened c. 250 BCE at the Asokarama monastery in Pataliputra under the patronage of Emperor Ashoka.
  • Presidency: Presided over by the senior monk Moggaliputta Tissa.
  • Purpose: To purge the Sangha of heretics, opportunistic monks, and false doctrines, re-establishing Theravada (Vibhajjavada) orthodoxy.
  • Kathavatthu & Canon: Moggaliputta Tissa composed the Kathavatthu ("Points of Controversy"), which was integrated into the canon as part of the Abhidhamma Pitaka.
  • Dispatch of Dharmadutas: Dispatched 9 missionary delegations across the world, including Mahendra and Sanghamitra to Sri Lanka.

29. Later Mauryas, Fragmentation & Decline (c. 185 BCE)

Following Ashoka’s death in c. 232 BCE, the empire rapidly fragmented. The Vishnu Purana preserves lists of later rulers. Later traditions (especially Jaina) describe western territories governed by Kunala and his son Samprati, while eastern centers were held by Dasharatha.

In c. 185 BCE (correct date, NOT 188 BCE), the last Mauryan emperor, Brihadratha, was assassinated during a military parade by his Brahmin commander-in-chief, Pushyamitra Shunga, who founded the Shunga dynasty.

UPSC Trap — Cause of Collapse

Do NOT treat a simplistic narrative that "the empire collapsed in 180 BCE due to Indo-Greek invasions" as established fact. Indo-Greek expansion in the north-west occurred within the broader context of post-Mauryan regional fragmentation.

Four Historiographical Theories of Decline

  • Brahminical Reaction (Haraprasad Sastri): Argued that Ashoka's ban on animal sacrifices and Dhamma-Mahamatras alienated Brahmins. Critique: Overstated; Ashoka repeatedly protected Brahmanas.
  • Ashoka's Pacifism (H.C. Raychaudhuri): Claimed military spirit decayed after Kalinga. Critique: Army was never disbanded and frontier tribes were sternly warned.
  • Fiscal / Economic Crisis (D.D. Kosambi): Debasement of later punch-marked silver coins points to severe economic exhaustion caused by maintaining an enormous bureaucracy and army.
  • Hyper-Centralisation & Weak Successors (Romila Thapar): Modern consensus: extreme centralization around the King's personal vigilance proved fatal when weak rulers succeeded Ashoka.

  • ✓ Revenue: Sannidhata (treasury) vs. Samaharta; 7 heads (Durga, Rashtra, etc.); Bhaga standard 1/6th (1/8th Lumbini)
  • ✓ Coinage: Predominantly silver punch-marked Panas, with copper punch-marked coins also attested (not silver only)
  • ✓ Slavery Laws (2022 PYQ): Megasthenes wrong; 9 types of Dasa; female slave bearing master's son is freed
  • ✓ Sudarsana Lake: Built under Chandragupta (Pushyagupta), canals under Ashoka (Tushaspha), repaired by Rudradaman I
  • ✓ Ashokan Pillars vs. Persian: Monolithic, smooth, free-standing without bases vs. segmented, fluted, structural
  • ✓ Stupa Architecture: Anda, Harmika, Chhatra, Torana; Sanchi toranas added under Satavahanas (not Ashoka)
  • ✓ Lomas Rishi Cave: Entrance imitates wooden barrel-vaulted prototype; proves transition from wood to stone
  • ✓ Didarganj Yakshi: Chauri bearer in polished sandstone; dating to Mauryan era debated by scholars
  • ✓ Popular Terracotta Art: Mother-goddess figurines, animals, folk traditions alongside court art
  • ✓ Sisupalgarh: Planned fortified early historic urban settlement in Odisha (NBPW, iron, punch-marked coins)
  • ✓ Contemporary China: Qin Shi Huang (221–210 BCE) connected earlier northern defensive walls, not built from scratch
  • ✓ Later Mauryas & End: Fragmentation; Kunala & Samprati; Brihadratha slain by Pushyamitra Shunga c. 185 BCE
