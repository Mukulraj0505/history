The Stone Age: Old Stone Age & New Stone Age

Palaeolithic Hunter-Gatherers, Mesolithic Microliths & Rock Art, The Neolithic Revolution, Regional Traditions, Sedentism & Ashmounds

UPSC Relevance & Analytical Framework

UPSC Core Perspective:

Do not study Indian Prehistory merely as a dry recitation of chronological dates or isolated site catalogues. The UPSC Civil Services Examination repeatedly interrogates the deep causal linkages connecting climatic transition (Pleistocene → Holocene) → raw material availability → tool technology (core/flake → microlith → ground stone/celt) → subsistence strategy (big game hunting → broad-spectrum foraging → food production & pastoralism) → settlement patterns (caves/rock-shelters → semi-sedentary camps → mud-brick villages) → symbolic cognition (rock art, burials, grave goods).

The transition from food gathering to food production (The Neolithic Revolution) forms the single most important conceptual thread uniting the entire chapter.

High-Yield Prelims Focus — Key Facts & Trap Zone

1. Common Prelims Trap Zone — Scrutinize Absolute Statements

Trap 1: "The Palaeolithic Age witnessed the origin of agriculture in India."

❌ Incorrect: Palaeolithic subsistence was based purely on hunting and gathering. Systematic agriculture developed only in the Neolithic Age (with transitional experimental gathering in the Mesolithic).

Trap 2: "Microliths are exclusive to the Neolithic period."

❌ Incorrect: Microliths are the hallmark of the Mesolithic Age. While microlithic blade technology continued into later Neolithic and Chalcolithic periods, they did not originate in the Neolithic.

Trap 3: "Burzahom in Kashmir is known for ancient rock-cut shrines." (UPSC 2021 PYQ)

❌ Incorrect: Burzahom is celebrated for Neolithic subterranean pit dwellings, bone tools, and dog burials. Rock-cut shrines belong to historic architectural traditions (such as Barabar or Ellora).

Trap 4: "Mehrgarh is an urban Harappan city with pit dwellings."

❌ Incorrect: Mehrgarh (Balochistan) is a pre-Harappan Neolithic-Chalcolithic agricultural settlement (c. 7000 BCE) with rectangular mud-brick structures and compartmented granaries. Pit dwellings are typical of Kashmir (Burzahom, Gufkral).

Trap 5: "Southern Indian ashmounds represent defensive military fortifications."

❌ Incorrect: Ashmounds (e.g., Utnur, Kupgal, Budihal) are archaeological accumulations produced by the periodic, high-temperature ritual/seasonal burning of accumulated cattle dung in pastoral penning encampments.

Trap 6: "The Neolithic Age was characterized by the widespread use of copper tools." (UPSC 1980 PYQ)

❌ Incorrect: The Neolithic relied exclusively on ground and polished stone tools (celts, axes, adzes). Copper metallurgy defines the subsequent Chalcolithic Age.

Trap 7: "Bhimbetka rock art belongs exclusively to the Palaeolithic period."

❌ Incorrect: Bhimbetka preserves a continuous sequence spanning Upper Palaeolithic, Mesolithic, Chalcolithic, and Early Historic periods. The overwhelming majority and richest artistic expressions belong to the Mesolithic.

Trap 8: "The Neolithic transition happened uniformly and simultaneously across all regions of India."

❌ Incorrect: The transition was multi-linear, gradual, and highly asynchronous: Mehrgarh entered farming by c. 7000 BCE, Belan Valley by c. 6000–5000 BCE, South India by c. 2500 BCE, and Kashmir by c. 2400 BCE.

Trap 9: "Hathnora yielded fossilized skeletal remains of Homo sapiens sapiens."

❌ Incorrect: The Hathnora skull cap discovered by Arun Sonakia (1982) is attributed to Homo erectus or archaic Homo sapiens (Narmada Man)—the only pre-Homo sapiens hominin fossil found in the subcontinent.

Trap 10: "The earliest evidence of rice in the subcontinent is confined exclusively to Koldihwa."

❌ Incorrect: Recent excavations at Lahuradewa (Sant Kabir Nagar, UP) have provided carbonized rice grains dated to c. 7000–6000 BCE, proving indigenous and very early Gangetic rice domestication.

Trap 11: "Daojali Hading in Assam yielded jadeite stone tools of indigenous local origin."

❌ Incorrect: Jadeite does not occur naturally in Assam; it was brought through prehistoric trans-Himalayan exchange from Yunnan (Southwest China) or Upper Myanmar.

Trap 12: "Robert Bruce Foote was an epigraphist who deciphered Ashokan edicts."

❌ Incorrect: Robert Bruce Foote was a geologist/archaeologist known as the Father of Indian Prehistory who discovered India's first Palaeolithic handaxe at Pallavaram (1863). James Prinsep deciphered Brahmi in 1837.

Trap 13: "The Holocene epoch began approximately 10,000 years ago."

❌ Incorrect: The formally defined beginning of the Holocene epoch is approximately 11,700 years ago (c. 9700 BCE), rather than the older rounded textbook figure of 10,000 years.

Trap 14: "The human evolutionary lineage originated during the Pleistocene epoch."

❌ Incorrect: The hominin lineage is much older (Miocene/Pliocene epochs, e.g. Australopithecus). The Pleistocene contains the major archaeological history of the genus Homo (H. erectus, H. sapiens) and stone tool evolution.

Trap 15: "Habitual bipedalism began with modern Homo sapiens."

❌ Incorrect: Habitual bipedalism evolved millions of years earlier in Australopithecus and early members of Homo; efficient upright walking preceded major brain expansion by a wide margin.

Trap 16: "Bori in Maharashtra has been definitively established as 1.4 million years old."

❌ Incorrect: Bori is an important early Acheulean locality, but the 1.4 Ma date remains controversial and unresolved; recent archaeological reviews treat it as an open chronological debate.

Trap 17: "Mehrgarh was unquestionably the first Neolithic settlement in the world, firmly dated to 7000 BCE."

❌ Incorrect: Mehrgarh is one of the earliest farming settlements in South Asia. While traditionally assigned to the 7th millennium BCE, a major 2025 radiocarbon reassessment proposed a date of ~5200 BCE for Period I, making its chronology an active archaeological debate.

Trap 18: "Gufkral is the Kashmir Neolithic site famous for dog burials with humans."

❌ Incorrect: Human burials accompanied by domesticated dog skeletons are the unique hallmark of Burzahom, NOT Gufkral.

Trap 19: "Cord-impressed pottery was manufactured by all Neolithic cultures across India."

❌ Incorrect: Cord-marked ware is a regional ceramic tradition diagnostic of the Belan/Vindhyan valley (Koldihwa) and the North-Eastern hills (Daojali Hading), not a universal feature of all Indian Neolithic sites.

Trap 20: "Black-and-Red Ware (BRW) is an exclusive hallmark of the Late Harappan culture."

❌ Incorrect: Black-and-Red Ware is a widespread multi-period ceramic tradition occurring across Neolithic, Chalcolithic (Ahar-Banas), Megalithic, and early Iron Age contexts across India.

Trap 21: "South Indian ashmounds were purely religious monuments constructed for fire sacrifices."

❌ Incorrect: Ashmounds were formed primarily by the burning of accumulated cattle dung inside pastoral penning enclosures, serving combined economic, pastoral, sanitary, and communal/ritual purposes.

1. Big Picture — Prehistoric India & Chronological Spectrum

Prehistory refers to that vast phase of the human past for which no written records exist. The reconstruction of prehistoric human life depends exclusively on material archaeological remains: stone tools, animal bones, macro-botanical floral seeds, stratigraphic habitation layers, pit dwellings, burials, pottery, and rock art.

Foundational Historiographical Fact:

The study of Indian prehistory began on 30 May 1863, when Robert Bruce Foote (British geologist of the Geological Survey of India) discovered a chipped quartzite Acheulian handaxe at Pallavaram near Madras (Chennai). For this pioneering breakthrough, Foote is universally revered as the "Father of Indian Prehistory".

1.1 Geological Epochs of the Quaternary Period

Human prehistory unfolded entirely within the Quaternary Period, which geologists divide into two distinct epochs:

Geological Epoch Chronological Boundary Climatic Environment Archaeological Association
Pleistocene Epoch ~2.58 Ma to 11,700 years ago The "Great Ice Age"; extensive continental glaciation, cold oscillations, fluctuating pluvial (rainy) and inter-pluvial phases. Corresponds to the Palaeolithic Age (Lower, Middle, and Upper Palaeolithic hunter-gatherers).
Holocene Epoch ~11,700 years ago (c. 9700 BCE) to Present Post-glacial global warming; retreat of ice sheets; higher rainfall and humidity; dense floral and faunal expansion. Corresponds to the Mesolithic, Neolithic, Chalcolithic, and subsequent historic civilisations.
Geological & Chronological Trap: The formally defined boundary of the Holocene is approximately 11,700 years ago, rather than the older rounded figure of 10,000 years. Furthermore, stating that "humans first appeared in the Pleistocene" is too broad—the hominin evolutionary lineage extends into the Pliocene epoch, though the genus Homo developed primarily during the Pleistocene.

1.2 Human Evolutionary Framework in Prehistoric Context

Human biological evolution was a gradual branching process rather than a simplistic linear ladder:

Hominin Group Key Anatomical & Behavioral Hallmarks Prehistoric Archaeological Significance
Australopithecus Habitual terrestrial bipedalism (upright walking); relatively small cranial capacity (400–500 cc). Bipedalism preceded major brain enlargement by millions of years; foundational hominin stage in Africa.
Homo habilis ("Handy Man") Enlarged brain (~650 cc); reduced dentition; earliest systematic manufacture of chipped pebble tools. Associated with the earliest Oldowan pebble-tool industry (c. 2.6–1.8 Ma).
Homo erectus ("Upright Man") Modern human-like body proportions; efficient long-distance striding bipedalism; brain capacity (900–1100 cc); controlled use of fire. Pioneered the Acheulean bifacial handaxe tradition; first major hominin dispersal beyond Africa into Eurasia. Attirampakkam and Hathnora in India relate to this horizon.
Homo sapiens (Modern Humans) High vaulted cranium (1350 cc); prominent chin; symbolic cognition, complex language, art, and composite tools. Responsible for the Upper Palaeolithic blade-and-burin culture, rock art (Bhimbetka), and subsequent Neolithic transformation.
Evolutionary Context for Indian Prehistory: Australopithecus, Homo habilis, and early Homo erectus are principally part of the wider African and Eurasian evolutionary record. They should not automatically be treated as populations identified at every Indian Stone Age site. Except for the Hathnora cranium ("Narmada Man"), Indian prehistoric sites are classified almost entirely through their lithic tool typologies rather than hominin skeletal fossils.
1. PALEOLITHIC (Old Stone Age | ~2.6 Ma to 11,700 BP / ~9700 BCE) Pleistocene epoch (Ice Age); nomadic hunter-gatherers; heavy chipped stone tools (handaxes, cleavers, choppers, flakes, blades).
2. MESOLITHIC (Middle Stone Age | ~11,700 BP to 6000/4000 BCE) Holocene epoch (warmer, humid climate); broad-spectrum hunting-gathering-fishing; invention of microliths; early animal domestication experiments; peak of prehistoric rock art.
3. NEOLITHIC (New Stone Age | ~7000 BCE to 1000 BCE) The "Neolithic Revolution"; systematic food production (cultivation of wheat, barley, rice); animal domestication; ground/polished stone celts; village sedentism; pottery and granaries.
4. CHALCOLITHIC (Stone-Copper Phase | ~2500 BCE to 700 BCE) First metallurgical phase; copper tools coexisting with stone blades; non-urban agro-pastoral cultures (Ahar-Banas, Kayatha, Malwa, Jorwe).
UPSC Golden Principle — Non-Linearity & Regional Diversity:

These prehistoric stages must never be visualized as a single uniform, rigid, all-India conveyor belt occurring simultaneously across the subcontinent. While Mehrgarh in Balochistan entered the Neolithic food-producing phase by c. 7000 BCE, hunting-gathering communities using Mesolithic microliths continued in central India and Rajasthan alongside pastoral and early farming communities. Prehistory in India is fundamentally a mosaic of regional trajectories shaped by local ecology.

2. Old Stone Age — Palaeolithic Period (~2.6 Ma – 10,000 BCE)

The Palaeolithic Age corresponds geologically to the Pleistocene Epoch (characterized by widespread glaciation, cold climate, and fluctuating pluvial/inter-pluvial periods). Early humans in India lived as completely mobile, nomadic hunter-gatherers who subsisted on wild plant foraging and hunting animals.

Core Epistemological Truth: Palaeolithic people possessed no knowledge of agriculture, no domesticated cattle/sheep, no house construction, and no pottery. They lived under open skies, rock shelters, and natural caves, using chipped quartzite, chert, jasper, and chalcedony stone tools. Because quartzite was the most common raw material, early Palaeolithic humans in India are often termed "Quartzite Men".

2.1 Tripartite Subdivisions of the Indian Palaeolithic

Based on progressive refinement in stone tool-making technology, tool typology, and geological stratigraphy, the Indian Palaeolithic is classified into three sequential phases:

2.2 Attirampakkam — Why It Rewrote Indian Prehistoric Chronology

Attirampakkam (Kortallayar River Basin, Tamil Nadu):

Excavated extensively by Shanti Pappu and Kumar Akhilesh (Sharma Centre for Heritage Education), Attirampakkam is one of the most critical archaeological sites in world prehistory:

  • Ancient Lower Palaeolithic Date: Using Cosmogenic Nuclide Burial Dating (Beryllium-10 and Aluminium-26), the Acheulian stone tools (handaxes, cleavers) were dated to approximately 1.51 ± 0.07 million years ago (Ma). This proved that hominins manufacturing Acheulian bifaces arrived in South Asia far earlier than older colonial textbooks had claimed.
  • Early Middle Palaeolithic Shift: Luminescence dating (OSL) of Middle Palaeolithic levels at the site showed that prepared-core Levallois flake technologies emerged by roughly 385,000 years ago (385 ka)—contemporaneous with similar technological transitions in Africa and Europe, challenging the outdated theory that Indian prehistory was a stagnant, backward backwater.

2.3 Hathnora & The "Narmada Man" — Only Indian Prehistoric Hominin Fossil

Hathnora Discovery (Sehore District, Narmada Valley, MP):

On 5 December 1982, geologist Arun Sonakia discovered a fossilized partial hominin cranium (skull cap) embedded in the basal gravels of the Narmada river at Hathnora.

Popularly christened "Narmada Man", palaeoanthropological analysis revealed it belonged to an adult female hominin with an estimated cranial capacity of ~1150–1400 cc. Taxonomically classified as late Homo erectus or archaic Homo sapiens, it remains to this day the only Pleistocene hominin cranial fossil ever discovered in the entire Indian subcontinent.

2.4 Bori (Maharashtra) — Early Acheulean & Chronological Debate

Bori Profile (Kukdi River Valley, Pune District, Maharashtra):

Bori is an internationally famous Lower Palaeolithic Acheulean site discovered in western Maharashtra:

  • Early Historical Claim (~1.4 Ma): Early potassium-argon dating of volcanic tephra (ash) beds associated with Acheulean stone tools initially yielded a startling age of approximately 1.4 million years ago, making Bori the candidate for India's oldest stone-tool site.
  • Contemporary Archaeological Reassessment: Subsequent geomorphological and tephra-provenance analyses (linking the ash to the Toba super-eruptions in Sumatra) disputed the early date, with alternative estimates suggesting ages under 670,000 BP or younger.
  • UPSC-Safe Formulation: "Bori is an important early Acheulean site in Maharashtra historically cited for very early Lower Palaeolithic evidence; its precise absolute chronology remains contested and subject to ongoing geological debate." Do not memorise 1.4 Ma as an uncontested fact.

2.5 Belan Valley (Uttar Pradesh) — Continuous Prehistoric Stratigraphy & Fauna

Belan Valley Prehistoric Sequence (Southern UP / Vindhyan Edge):

Located in Prayagraj (Allahabad) and Mirzapur districts along the Belan river, this valley provides the single most comprehensive geological and archaeological cross-section in northern India:

  • Continuous Multi-Period Sequence: Preserves uninterrupted stratigraphic layers progressing from Lower Palaeolithic → Middle Palaeolithic → Upper Palaeolithic → Mesolithic → Neolithic (Koldihwa and Mahagara).
  • Pleistocene Animal Fossils: Yielded an extraordinary wealth of fossilised animal skeletal remains (wild cattle / Bos namadicus, wild buffalo, antelope, deer, elephant, hippopotamus), providing vital evidence for reconstructing the faunal ecology and subsistence patterns of prehistoric hunter-gatherers.

2.6 Acheulian vs Soanian Traditions — Regional Lower Palaeolithic Dichotomy

Analytical Parameter Acheulian Tradition (Madrasian) Soanian Tradition (Pebble-Tool)
Geographical Epicentre Peninsular India, Central India, Chota Nagpur, Gujarat, Rajasthan. Classic site: Attirampakkam (near Madras). North-Western subcontinent; Soan River valley (Potwar Plateau, Rawalpindi, Pakistan). Identified by H. de Terra and T.T. Paterson (1939).

2.7 Upper Palaeolithic Innovations & Pan-Indian Regional Distribution

Lohanda Nala (Belan Valley, UP) Yielded a carved bone artefact dated to ~20,000 BP, traditionally identified as a "Mother Goddess figurine" and alternatively as a barbed bone harpoon.
Patne (Jalgaon, Maharashtra) Excavated by S.A. Sali; yielded over 40 engraved ostrich eggshell pieces and beads dated to ~25,000 BP, proving Pleistocene ostriches and early symbolic art.

Upper Palaeolithic Chronology Caution: Do not attach a single rigid "20,000–10,000 BCE" bracket to all Upper Palaeolithic sites across India. Blade technologies emerged asynchronously across Maharashtra, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, Madhya Pradesh, Uttar Pradesh, and Bihar.

3. Mesolithic Period — The Holocene Transitional Bridge (~10,000 – 6000/4000 BCE)

The Mesolithic Age (Middle Stone Age) was triggered by a profound climatic revolution: the end of the Pleistocene Ice Age and the onset of the Holocene Epoch around 10,000 BCE. The climate became substantially warmer, more humid, and rainfall increased, leading to the dense growth of tropical flora, fauna, and aquatic resources.

Ecological Transformation & Broad-Spectrum Foraging:

Faced with fast-moving smaller animals (deer, gazelles, birds, hares) and rich riverine/lake aquatic life, humans shifted from hunting massive megafauna to broad-spectrum foraging, small game hunting, fowling, and intensive fishing.

3.1 Microliths — The Defining Technological Breakthrough

The hallmark of the Mesolithic is the Microlith—tiny, exquisitely shaped stone tools measuring from 1 cm to 5 cm in length, manufactured on micro-blades of fine cryptocrystalline silica (chert, chalcedony, agate, carnelian, jasper, quartz):

  • Geometric Microliths: Trapezes, triangles, lunates (crescents), rhomboids.
  • Non-Geometric Microliths: Micro-blades, points, borers, scrapers, burins.
  • Composite Tool Technology (Hafting): Microliths were rarely used in the bare hand; they were slotted and hafted into grooves on wooden or bone handles using natural tree resins and bitumen to create serrated sickles, barbed spearheads, harpoons, and arrows (the invention of the bow and arrow).

3.3 Prehistoric Rock Art — Especially Bhimbetka (UNESCO World Heritage)

Bhimbetka Caves (Vindhyan Range, Raisen District, MP):

Discovered in 1957 by Indian archaeologist V.S. Wakankar, Bhimbetka features over 750 rock shelters spread across 10 km, of which more than 400 contain paintings:

  • Continuous Cultural Spectrum: Paintings span Upper Palaeolithic, Mesolithic, Chalcolithic, Early Historic, and Medieval periods. The Mesolithic phase represents the zenith in artistic vibrancy, thematic variety, and stylistic dynamism.
  • Natural Pigments & Colors: Artists used minerals ground into powder and mixed with water, tree resin, or animal fat. Red came from iron oxide (hematite / geru); green from chlorite / chalcedony rock; white from limestone or kaolin; black from manganese or charcoal.
  • Subject Matter: Vivid hunting scenes (hunters armed with barbed spears, bows, and arrows chasing bisons, boars, tigers, stags, rhinoceroses), community round dances, family rituals, pregnant animals (x-ray depictions), women grinding grain, child burials, and mythical horned beasts.
  • Socio-Cultural Insight for GS-I Mains: Rock art demonstrates that Mesolithic humans possessed symbolic cognition, division of labour, ritual awareness, magical beliefs for hunting success, and profound aesthetic sensibilities.

4. New Stone Age — The Neolithic Revolution (~7000 – 1000 BCE)

Definition of the Neolithic: The term Neolithic (derived from Greek neos = new, lithos = stone) designates the revolutionary stage where human groups ceased to be passive parasites on nature (food gatherers) and actively transformed into food producers through the deliberate cultivation of domesticable plants and the selective breeding of animals.

4.1 Why Did V. Gordon Childe Call It a "Revolution"?

In 1936, Australian prehistorian Vere Gordon Childe coined the phrase "Neolithic Revolution" (Man Makes Himself) to describe the monumental socio-economic transformation wrought by agriculture:

Plant & Animal Domestication
→
Permanent Agrarian Sedentism
→
Storage of Agricultural Surplus
→

Modern Archaeological Qualification (Mains Nuance):

Contemporary archaeologists emphasize that the "revolution" was not a sudden, overnight event. It was a centuries-long evolutionary process ("Neolithisation") characterized by trial-and-error plant management, continuing reliance on hunting and foraging, and stark regional asynchronous development. Sedentism also brought biological stresses: increased disease transmission, dental caries from starch diets, and arduous repetitive labour.

4.2 Core Technological Markers of the Neolithic

Neolithic Tool / Artefact Technological Description Functional & Historical Significance
Polished Stone Celt (Axe) Fine-grained basalt/dolerite ground, pecked, and polished on stone querns to create sharp, resilient cutting edges. Felling hardwood forests, clearing agricultural plots, carpentry, canoe building, and tillage. Unlike chipped tools, polished edges do not shatter on impact.
Adze & Chisel Bevelled ground stone tools with cutting edges transverse to the handle. Woodworking, carving wooden bowls, shaping digging sticks, and construction of timber-frame huts.
Perforated Stones / Ring Stones Heavy circular stones with a central drilled hole (mace-heads). Used as weights on digging sticks to penetrate hard dry soil for planting tubers and seeds; also served as mace-head weapons.
Saddle Querns & Mullers Flat or concave stone slabs (querns) with matching rolling pestles (mullers/grinders). Crushing and grinding cereal grains (wheat, barley, millets) into flour—the undeniable physical proof of agricultural processing.
Pottery (Cord-Marked, Burnished) Initially handmade and basket-impressed; later wheel-thrown; fired in open pits. Cooking cereal porridge and stews; storing surplus grain, milk, and water; protecting seeds against rodents and moisture.

5. Regional Neolithic Traditions of India — The Six Great Clusters

One of the most essential concepts tested by UPSC is that there was no single monolithic Neolithic culture in India. Instead, six distinct geographic regions evolved specialized subsistence strategies adapted to their local ecology:

Neolithic Region Key Representative Sites Crops & Subsistence Base Distinctive Archaeological Signatures
1. North-Western Frontier Mehrgarh, Kili Gul Mohammad, Rana Ghundai (Balochistan) Wheat (emmer, einkorn), two-row barley; domesticated sheep, goat, zebu cattle; cotton. Earliest farming village (c. 7000 BCE); multi-room mud-brick houses; compartmented granaries; in vivo dental drilling.
2. Northern / Kashmir Valley Burzahom, Gufkral, Kanispur Barley, wheat, lentils; sheep, goat, cattle; hunting of stag and ibex. Subterranean pit dwellings; profusion of bone tools; perforated stone harvesters; dog buried with human master; coarse grey pottery.
3. Central & Middle Gangetic Koldihwa, Mahagara, Chopani Mando (Belan), Lahuradewa (UP) Rice (Oryza sativa), barley; domesticated cattle, sheep, goats. Cord-marked pottery (corded ware); Lahuradewa very early rice (c. 7000–6000 BCE); Mahagara circular huts & cattle pen with hoof impressions.
4. Eastern India Chirand (Bihar), Pandu Rajar Dhibi (Bengal), Kuchai (Odisha), Barudih (Jharkhand) Rice, wheat, barley, pulses; fish, deer, cattle. Extraordinary abundance of deer-antler and bone tools at Chirand; circular wattle-and-daub huts; polished celts.
5. North-Eastern Hills Daojali Hading, Sarutaru (Assam), Selbalgre (Garo Hills, Meghalaya) Shifting hoe cultivation (jhum); yams, taro, millets. Faceted ground stone celts; cord-marked ceramics; imported jadeite stone tools sourced from Yunnan / Upper Myanmar.
6. Southern Peninsular Utnur, Piklihal, Budihal, Kupgal/Sanganakallu, Hallur, Tekkalakota, Brahmagiri Millets (ragi / finger millet, horse gram / kulthi); heavy cattle pastoralism. Signature ashmounds (accumulated vitrified burnt cow-dung); hilltop terraced habitations; rock bruisings of humped bulls.

5.1 Mehrgarh (Balochistan) — Pioneer of South Asian Agriculture & Chronological Reassessment

Mehrgarh Archaeological Sequence (Kachi Plain, Bolan Pass, Pakistan):

Located in the Kachi Plain of Balochistan, present-day Pakistan, near the strategic Bolan Pass, Mehrgarh was excavated by French archaeologist Jean-François Jarrige (1974 onwards). It is one of the earliest and most important farming settlements in South Asia, with evidence for cultivation, animal management, and mud-brick architecture:

  • Period I (Aceramic / Pre-Pottery Neolithic): Completely aceramic (no pottery). Settlers lived in rectangular multi-room mud-brick houses; constructed compartmented storehouses/granaries; domesticated emmer and einkorn wheat, six-row barley, sheep, goat, and humped zebu cattle. Physical anthropologists discovered unambiguous proof of dental drilling on live human teeth using micro-flint head drills.
  • Period II (Ceramic Neolithic): Introduction of handmade and wheel-turned pottery; discovery of cotton seeds (earliest evidence of cotton cultivation in the Old World); glazed steatite beads and terracotta female figurines.
  • Significance for UPSC: Mehrgarh disproved the colonial diffusionist myth that agriculture was imported into India from Western Asia (Fertile Crescent), establishing an independent primary centre of domestication in South Asia.
  • The 2025 Chronological Reassessment & Ongoing Debate: Traditional archaeological chronology places the earliest occupation around the 7th millennium BCE (~7000 BCE). However, a major radiocarbon reassessment published in 2025 proposed a substantially later date, around 5200 BCE, for the earliest securely dated Period I material. This represents a significant chronological revision and should be treated as an ongoing archaeological debate.
UPSC-Safe Approach: Do not write "Mehrgarh is unquestionably the first Neolithic settlement of the world." Write: "Mehrgarh is one of the earliest known farming settlements of South Asia." For conventional UPSC preparation, retain the traditional association with the 7th millennium BCE, while demonstrating awareness that its absolute chronology is being actively reassessed.

5.2 Burzahom & Gufkral (Kashmir) — Pit Dwellers, Bone Tools & Human-Animal Burials

Burzahom (Place of Birch Trees, near Srinagar):
  • Subterranean Pit Dwellings: Circular and oval pits dug into the Karewa silt beds, wider at the base and narrower at the mouth, featuring stepped entrances, post-holes for birch/thatched conical roofs, and hearths inside—designed to protect against harsh Himalayan winters.
  • Bone Tool Industry: Phenomenal variety of polished bone implements: harpoons for fishing, needles for stitching hides, awls, daggers, and arrowheads.
  • Perforated Stone Knives ("Harvesters"): Rectangular semi-lunar stone knives with two drilled holes, unique in India and showing trans-Himalayan contact with the Yangshao Neolithic culture of North China.
  • Distinctive Human and Animal Burials: Both human and animal burials are attested at Burzahom. Humans were buried in flexed positions; unique graves contained skeletons of domesticated dogs and wolves buried along with their human masters in the site's Neolithic sequence; trepanned human skulls (cranial trepanation).
Gufkral ("Cave of the Potter", Pulwama District, Kashmir Valley):

Excavated by A.K. Sharma, Gufkral is another important Neolithic site forming part of the broader Kashmir Neolithic tradition along with Burzahom. It provides rich evidence of agriculture, animal domestication (sheep, goat, cattle), and Neolithic settlement activity, progressing through an Aceramic Neolithic into mature ceramic phases.

Prelims Trap & Correction: The association of a dog burial should be made primarily with Burzahom, NOT used as a defining feature of Gufkral. Gufkral is important for the wider Kashmir Neolithic tradition, but Burzahom is the standard UPSC association for distinctive human–animal burial practices.

5.3 Belan Valley & Middle Ganga — Ceramics & The Early Rice Domestication Debate

Belan Valley Regional Sequence (Uttar Pradesh → Vindhyan / Middle Ganga Region):

The Belan Valley preserves a remarkably long, continuous prehistoric stratigraphic sequence extending from the Lower Palaeolithic through the Mesolithic to the Neolithic. Early Neolithic levels at Koldihwa are associated with a distinctive ceramic suite:

  • Cord-Marked / Cord-Impressed Pottery: The primary diagnostic marker, produced by wrapping twisted cords around clay paddles during vessel formation.
  • Incised Pottery: Decorated with geometric incised patterns.
  • Plain Red Ware: Functional utilitarian ceramics.
  • Coarse Forms of Black-and-Red Ware (BRW): Demonstrating that early versions of BRW existed long before Late Harappan or Iron Age times.
Correction on Pottery: The statement "cord-impressed pottery was used by all Neolithic cultures" is too broad. The association is particularly useful in the context of specific archaeological traditions such as Koldihwa and eastern/northeastern complexes.
Koldihwa & Mahagara (Belan Valley, UP) Traditionally cited for very early evidence of rice cultivation. However, the extremely early dates once assigned to this evidence have been questioned. Archaeobotanical studies show that the history of rice domestication in the Ganga–Vindhyan region was more complex than the older "first domesticated rice" formulation suggested.
Lahuradewa (Sant Kabir Nagar, Middle Ganga, UP) Excavated by Rakesh Tewari; yielded carbonized domesticated rice grains dated via AMS to c. 7000–6000 BCE, placing the Middle Ganga Plain at the center of ongoing global debates regarding independent rice domestication.
Mahagara (Belan Valley, UP) Showed a true pastoral-village layout: 20 circular wattle-and-daub huts arranged around a central cattle pen with hardened clay floor preserving hundreds of cattle hoof impressions.
Chopani Mando (Belan Valley, UP) Stratigraphic transition from Late Upper Palaeolithic through Mesolithic to early farming; wild rice gatherers living in wattle-and-daub beehive huts.
UPSC-Safe Wording on Early Rice:
"Koldihwa–Mahagara provide important early evidence associated with rice cultivation in the Belan Valley, although the precise antiquity and degree of domestication of the earliest rice remains debated."

5.4 Southern Peninsular Neolithic — Cattle Pastoralism, Crops & Ashmounds

The Southern Neolithic (c. 3000–1000 BCE) evolved on the semi-arid granite plateaus and hilltops of Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh, and Telangana:

Site Region / State Important Archaeological Association
Piklihal Raichur, Karnataka Cattle pastoralism; Southern Neolithic terrace settlements; rock bruisings of humped bulls.
Brahmagiri Chitradurga, Karnataka Important multi-period site excavated by Mortimer Wheeler; unbroken stratigraphy: Neolithic → Megalithic → Early Historic sequence.
Maski Raichur, Karnataka Important Southern Neolithic settlement; later sequence overlaps with metal-using traditions; famous for Ashoka's Minor Rock Edict naming "Devanampriya Asoka".
Hallur Haveri, Karnataka Important Neolithic–Chalcolithic sequence; later copper/bronze evidence and earliest transition to the Iron Age (~1000 BCE).
Utnur, Budihal, Kupgal Andhra Pradesh & Karnataka Major ashmound sites; Budihal preserves butchering floor and community feasting areas.
Southern Neolithic Ecological Pattern & Crops:

Cattle keeping was particularly important, and several settlements were associated with cattle pens and ashmounds. Radiometric studies show that the chronology of the Southern Neolithic is considerably more nuanced than older fixed textbook dates suggest.

  • Ragi (Finger Millet): Became an indispensable drought-resilient staple crop in peninsular prehistoric agricultural systems.
  • Horse Gram (Kulthi): Extensively cultivated across the southern prehistoric agricultural zone.
  • Ecological Contrast: Wheat and barley are strongly associated with early north-western farming traditions such as Mehrgarh, while crop patterns in peninsular India were shaped by local semi-arid ecological conditions.
South Indian Ashmounds — Updated Understanding:

Southern Neolithic ashmounds are large accumulations produced largely through the burning of accumulated cattle dung, often associated with cattle-keeping and cattle-penning practices.

  • Debated Function: Their precise function remains debated. Scholarly interpretations include pastoral/economic activity, sanitation or disposal practices, and communal/ritual activity (seasonal pastoral festivals).
  • Vitrification: Burning took place at intense temperatures exceeding 800°C, producing scoriaceous glassy slag.
Ashmound Correction: Ashmounds should not be described simply as "religious mounds." Their formation and significance involve both economic/pastoral and possible ritual dimensions.

5.5 Prehistoric Pottery Traditions — UPSC Chronological & Regional Clarifications

Some prehistoric pottery traditions provide useful chronological and cultural clues, but a pottery type should not automatically be assigned to one entire historical period or culture across India.

Pottery / Tradition Technological Horizon UPSC-Safe Association
Cord-Marked / Cord-Impressed Ware Neolithic Occurs in several Neolithic contexts; especially useful diagnostic marker at sites such as Koldihwa and northeastern sites (Daojali Hading).
Black-and-Red Ware (BRW) Neolithic → Chalcolithic → Iron Age / Megalithic Widespread archaeological tradition occurring across multiple regions and periods; manufactured using inverted firing; not exclusive to Late Harappan culture.
Painted Grey Ware (PGW) Iron Age (~1100–600 BCE) Later archaeological tradition associated broadly with the Iron Age / early historic transition in northern India (Ganga-Yamuna Doab; Vedic connections).
Northern Black Polished Ware (NBPW) Early Historic (~600–200 BCE) Much later deluxe glossy ware, associated particularly with the second half of the first millennium BCE, the rise of the Mahajanapadas, and the Second Urbanisation.
Prelims Trap & Correction: Black-and-Red Ware ≠ only Late Harappan. It occurs across a wide geographical and chronological range, including Neolithic levels (Koldihwa), Chalcolithic cultures (Ahar-Banas), South Indian Megalithic cairns, and early Iron Age horizons.

7. UPSC PYQ Analysis — What UPSC Actually Tests

Prelims 1980
Which one of the following was NOT a characteristic feature of the Neolithic Age?
  1. Cultivation of cereals
  2. Domestication of animals
  3. Use of polished stone tools
  4. Use of copper tools
Correct Answer: D (Use of copper tools)

Analytical Insight & Lesson

UPSC tests period boundaries through negative identification. Copper metallurgy belongs strictly to the subsequent Chalcolithic Age. The Neolithic is purely a lithic (stone) technology phase characterized by ground/polished tools.

Prelims 1988
Humans shifted from the food-gathering stage to the food-producing stage during the:
  1. Palaeolithic Age
  2. Mesolithic Age
  3. Neolithic Age
  4. Chalcolithic Age
Correct Answer: C (Neolithic Age)

Analytical Insight & Lesson

Reinforces the fundamental economic definition of the Neolithic Revolution as coined by Gordon Childe: the decisive shift from hunting-gathering parasitic economy to agricultural food production.

Prelims 2009
At which of the following prehistoric sites is the earliest evidence of animal domestication in India found?
  1. Bagor and Adamgarh
  2. Burzahom and Gufkral
  3. Chirand and Koldihwa
  4. Sarai Nahar Rai and Mahadaha
Correct Answer: A (Bagor and Adamgarh)

Analytical Insight & Lesson

Both Bagor (Rajasthan) and Adamgarh (Madhya Pradesh) yielded faunal assemblages of domesticated cattle, sheep, and goats dating back to the Mesolithic period (c. 5000 BCE), representing the evolutionary prelude to full-fledged Neolithic farming.

Prelims 2021
Consider the following pairs (Historical place — Well-known for):
1. Burzahom — Rock-cut shrines
2. Chandraketugarh — Terracotta art
3. Inamgaon — Copper artefacts
Which of the pairs given above is/are correctly matched?
  1. 1 only
  2. 1 and 2
  3. 2 and 3
  4. 2 only
Correct Answer: C (or 2 and 3 in standard keys)

Analytical Insight & Lesson

Pair 1 is unambiguously FALSE: Burzahom is celebrated for Neolithic pit dwellings and bone tools, not rock-cut shrines. This is a classic UPSC trap where a famous prehistoric site is deceptively paired with an architectural feature belonging to a much later historic era.

Mains GS-I 2015
"Mesolithic rock architecture and paintings not only reflect the cultural life of the times but also a fine aesthetic sense comparable to modern times." Critically evaluate. (150 Words | 10 Marks)
Mains Model Framework

Structured Answer Architecture

• Introduction: Define Mesolithic rock art (Bhimbetka, Adamgarh, Mirzapur) as the earliest mirror of human cognition and social life during the Holocene transition.

• Cultural Life Reflected: Subsistence (hunting large/small game, honey gathering, fishing with barbed spears); division of labour (men hunting, women foraging and cooking); social rituals (community round dances with masked figures, childbirth, mourning); warfare/conflict.

• Aesthetic Sense: Dynamic movement of animals, naturalistic x-ray perspective, masterful economy of strokes, long-lasting mineral pigments (hematite, chlorite), utilization of natural rock contours to impart 3D depth.

• Conclusion: Mesolithic rock art transcended functional hunting magic; it represented the dawn of abstract symbolism, community solidarity, and sublime visual storytelling.

8. GS-I Mains Answer Writing Framework

8.1 The "Neolithic Revolution" Conceptual Matrix

1. CAUSE (Environmental & Demographic Push)

Holocene warming + extinction of Pleistocene megafauna + localized demographic pressure near perennial water bodies prompted intense exploitation of wild cereals and animal managing.

2. TECHNOLOGICAL INNOVATION (The Ground Stone Complex)

Invention of the ground and polished stone celt enabled heavy forest clearing; saddle querns processed coarse grains; ceramic pottery permitted boiling, cooking, and long-term storage.

3. STRUCTURAL IMPACT (Sedentism to Social Differentiation)

Agriculture anchored human bands to land → permanent mud-brick villages → food surplus enabled non-food producing artisans (potters, weavers) → emergence of private property concepts, lineage clans, and formal burials.

4. REGIONAL DIVERSITY & BIOLOGICAL TRADE-OFFS

Multi-centric evolution (Mehrgarh wheat/barley vs Gangetic rice vs Deccan millets); biological penalties included infectious diseases from close animal cohabitation, dental decay, and repetitive physical labour.

9. 30-Second Memory Formula & Conceptual Flow

• Palaeolithic: Chipped quartzite handaxes/cleavers (Attirampakkam 1.51 Ma, Pallavaram 1863) + Narmada Man skull (Hathnora 1982) + Pleistocene nomadic hunters.
