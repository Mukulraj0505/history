Central Asian Contacts & Their Results

UPSC Orientation & Syllabus Framework

UPSC Core Weightage — Interaction, Syncretism & Trans-Regional Commerce

The post-Mauryan epoch in northern and north-western India is defined by intense external contact and profound cultural synthesis rather than destructive invasions. UPSC repeatedly interrogates this chapter through four interconnected pillars: (1) Art & Iconography: Syncretic nature of the Gandhara School (tested in GS-I Mains 2014 & 2019) contrasting with the indigenous Mathura School; (2) Numismatics & Economy: The evolution from punch-marked currency to high-standard Indo-Greek portrait coinage and extensive Kushana gold and copper coinage linked to the Silk Route and Roman maritime trade; (3) Religious Evolution: Transformation of Buddhism under Kanishka, the emergence of Mahayana and Bodhisattva cults, and the Fourth Buddhist Council in Kashmir; and (4) Literature & Inscriptions: The Milinda-panha (tested in Prelims 2011 & 2023) and Rudradaman’s Junagadh Rock Inscription (150 CE) as milestones of early classical Sanskrit political discourse.

1. Core Idea of the Chapter

Central Asian Contacts = Political Entry + Commercial Integration + Cultural Synthesis

The post-Mauryan north-west became a dynamic interaction zone connecting the Indian subcontinent with Central Asia, Iran, the Mediterranean world, and the trans-continental Silk Routes. The decline of Mauryan political centralisation in the north-west created a political vacuum filled by a succession of foreign-origin ruling dynasties.

The major groups relevant to UPSC examination analysis are summarized below:

Power / Group Broad Period Key Historical Association & UPSC Focus
Indo-Greeks (Bactrian Greeks) c. 2nd – 1st century BCE Demetrius, Menander (Milinda), Milinda-panha, portrait and bilingual coins, first gold coinage in India.
Shakas (Scythians) From late 2nd / 1st century BCE Maues (Moga), Western Kshatrapas, Rudradaman I, Junagadh rock inscription, Sudarsana lake repair.
Indo-Parthians (Pahlavas) Mainly 1st century CE Gondophernes, north-western transitional authority, Christian tradition of St. Thomas.
Kushanas (Yuezhi branch) 1st – 3rd century CE Kujula Kadphises, Vima Kadphises, Kanishka, Silk Route control, Mahayana Buddhism, Gandhara & Mathura art.
UPSC Trap — Ethnic Distinction

Do not treat all these groups as culturally identical "Central Asian invaders".
• Indo-Greeks: Hellenistic rulers originating from the Graeco-Bactrian kingdom south of the Oxus.
• Shakas: Nomadic pastoral Scythian tribes originating from the steppes north of the Jaxartes/Syr Darya.
• Indo-Parthians: Peoples of Iranian Parthian extraction.
• Kushanas: One of the five clans of the nomadic Yuezhi confederacy originally displaced from the frontiers of western China.

2. The Indo-Greeks (Bactrian Greeks)

UPSC Historical Accuracy Formulation

The statement that Indo-Greeks were simply the "first Greeks to invade India" is historically too loose because Alexander the Great had already entered the north-west in 326 BCE. For UPSC examinations, the precise formulation is: Indo-Greeks established post-Alexandrian Hellenistic political rule in parts of north-western India.

Historical Background & Eastern Expansion

  • Following the weakening of the Seleucid Empire in the mid-3rd century BCE, the Greek province of Bactria (northern Afghanistan around Balkh) declared independence under Diodotus I.
  • In the early 2nd century BCE (c. 190 BCE), Demetrius I, son of Euthydemus of Bactria, marched into north-western India, taking advantage of the post-Mauryan political fragmentation.
  • The Indo-Greek kingdoms were politically fragmented into several regional principalities, with over thirty kings and queens known primarily through their prolific numismatic output.
  • Ganga–Yamuna Expansion: Indo-Greek influence extended considerably beyond the north-west. Traditional Indian accounts (such as the Yuga Purana and Patanjali’s Mahabhashya) and epigraphic traditions (e.g., Hathigumpha inscription) associate Indo-Greek military campaigns with areas as far east as the Ganga–Yamuna region, reaching Saketa and threatening Pataliputra.

Menander I (Milinda) & Buddhist Epigraphic Evidence

  • Menander I (c. 165/155 – 130 BCE) was the most celebrated and powerful Indo-Greek monarch.
  • His capital was located at Sakala (modern Sialkot in Punjab, Pakistan), an important commercial and cultural center described admiringly in Buddhist literature.
  • His realm extended from the Kabul valley across the Punjab, with coins discovered as far as Mathura and Bharuch.
  • The Milinda-panha ("Questions of King Milinda"): A famous philosophical dialogue in Pali recording the intellectual debates between King Menander and the erudite Buddhist sage Nagasena. Menander was deeply impressed by Nagasena's answers, converted to Buddhism as a lay disciple (Upasaka), and became a major patron of the Sangha.
  • Epigraphic Confirmation of Buddhist Patronage: A Kharoshthi inscription from the north-west (the famous Bajaur / Shinkot relic casket inscription in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa) records the enshrinement of sacred Buddhist relics during the reign of a king identified as Menander (Minadra), providing irrefutable material proof of Indo-Greek–Buddhist patronal interaction.
High-Yield UPSC Elimination Trap (Tested 2023 Prelims)

Milinda-panha is a dialogue between King Menander (Milinda) and the Buddhist monk Nagasena.
Do NOT confuse Nagasena with Nagarjuna! Nagarjuna was the later philosopher (c. 2nd century CE) who formulated the Madhyamaka school and Shunyavada. UPSC explicitly used this confusion as an elimination trap in Prelims 2023.

Decline & Regional Collapse of Indo-Greek Power

The Indo-Greek territories did not disappear simultaneously in a single stroke; their power in different regions ended at different times under mounting nomadic and Iranian pressure:

  • Hindu Kush Region: Hermaeus, one of the later Indo-Greek rulers, lost territories in the Hindu Kush and Kabul region in the face of Central Asian nomadic migrations and early Parthian pressure.
  • Gandhara: Indo-Greek control in Gandhara declined progressively under pressure from the Parthians and Shakas.
  • East of the Jhelum: In the Punjab plains east of the Jhelum, Indo-Greek political authority was gradually displaced and replaced by the Shakas / Kshatrapas (such as Rajuvula).

Indo-Greek Coinage — Extra Prelims Facts

  • Introduction of Recognisable Royal Portraits: Indo-Greek rulers were among the earliest rulers in India to issue coins carrying realistic, individual royal portraits alongside the name and title of the reigning monarch.
  • Bilingual & Biscriptal Legends: Their coins frequently carried Greek legends on the obverse (written left-to-right) and, in Indian territories, bilingual Greek–Kharosthi legends on the reverse (written right-to-left in Prakrit).
  • Adaptation to Indian Scripts (Brahmi): Some coins (such as issues of Agathocles and Pantaleon) also show the use of Brahmi script, demonstrating deliberate adaptation to regional Indian linguistic and administrative environments.
  • First Gold Coinage in India: Standard UPSC formulation: Indo-Greeks were the first rulers in India to issue gold coins, though their gold issues were relatively limited compared to the extensive commercial gold currencies later issued by the Kushanas.
  • Die-Striking Technique: Introduced sophisticated two-die striking methods, replacing the older punch-marking technique and producing coins with precise weights, sharp relief, and standardized shapes.

Other Cultural Contributions

  • Hellenistic Influence: Introduced Hellenistic artistic conventions into the north-west, contributing directly to the visual vocabulary from which the Gandhara School of Art subsequently emerged.
  • Astronomy & Astrology: Greek astronomy exerted significant influence on ancient Indian planetary sciences. The term Yavana (derived from Ionian Greeks) became synonymous in Sanskrit texts with foreign astronomers, celebrated in treatises like the Yavanajataka of Sphujidhvaja. Sanskrit astrological vocabulary absorbed Greek loanwords such as Hora (from Greek hora) and Drekana (from dekanos).
  • Curtain in Drama (Yavanika): The Sanskrit theatrical term for the curtain—Yavanika—is traditionally associated with the influence of Yavanas/Greeks on theatrical staging.

3. The Shakas / Scythians & Western Kshatrapas

Origins & Routes of Entry

The Shakas (known in Classical Greek accounts as Scythians) were nomadic pastoralists originating from the steppes of Central Asia north of the Jaxartes (Syr Darya). Displaced by the westward migration of the Yuezhi, they moved southward across the Oxus into Bactria, overran Sakastan (Seistan in eastern Iran), and entered the Indian subcontinent through the north-western passes. Movements through the Bolan Pass and the lower Indus region (traditionally designated in classical accounts as Indo-Scythia) were particularly prominent in establishing their foothold, ultimately displacing and absorbing Indo-Greek enclaves.

The Five Major Shaka Branches

Branch / Region Area of Power Key Historical Features & Significance
1. North-Western Branch Afghanistan & adjoining north-west Established early control along the Hindukush foothills; displaced Hellenistic frontier posts.
2. Punjab Branch Punjab (Seat: Taxila) Maues (Moga) (c. 90–60 BCE) was the earliest prominent Shaka monarch; issued copper and silver bilingual coins. Succeeded by Azes I and Azes II.
3. Mathura Branch Mathura (Yamuna valley) Governors titled Kshatrapas and Mahakshatrapas (e.g., Rajuvula, Sodasa); active interaction with Jainism, Buddhism, and Bhagavata traditions (Mathura Lion Capital).
4. Western Branch Western India (Gujarat, Malwa, Saurashtra) Most enduring Shaka power, known as the Western Kshatrapas. Divided into two houses: Kshaharata (Nahapana, later defeated by Gautamiputra Satakarni) and Kardamaka (Chashtana, Rudradaman I).
5. Southern Branch Parts of the Upper Deccan Held territory in the northern Maharashtra / upper Deccan belt, coming into recurring conflict and eventual matrimonial alliances with the Satavahanas.
UPSC Accuracy Qualification — Vikram Samvat (57 BCE)

• The Vikram Samvat era is conventionally reckoned from 57 BCE.
• Later Indian tradition associates its foundation with the victory of King Vikramaditya of Ujjain over the Shakas.
• UPSC Caution: The identification of a historical ruler named Vikramaditya who personally defeated the Shakas in 57 BCE is legendary/traditional and not securely established in contemporary epigraphy or numismatics. Do not present this in UPSC Prelims or Mains as an undisputed historical fact!

Rudradaman I (c. 130 – 150 CE) & The Junagadh Inscription

Rudradaman I was the most celebrated monarch of the Kardamaka dynasty of Western Kshatrapas, ruling over Saurashtra, Malwa, Kutch, northern Maharashtra, and the lower Indus valley.

  • The Junagadh / Girnar Rock Multi-Layer Epigraphs: Carved on a massive granite boulder at the foot of Mount Girnar in Junagadh (Gujarat). The rock preserves three successive imperial inscriptional layers:
    • 1. Ashoka Maurya (c. 250 BCE): 14 Major Rock Edicts in Prakrit (Brahmi script) — the earliest major inscriptional layer.
    • 2. Rudradaman I (150 CE): Western Kshatrapa panegyric in classical Sanskrit prose.
    • 3. Skandagupta (c. 456–458 CE): Gupta imperial inscription recording a subsequent restoration of the reservoir during his reign.
  • Milestone in Epigraphy & Wording Precision: Do not write simply that it was the "first Sanskrit inscription". A precise UPSC formulation is: Rudradaman’s Junagadh inscription is the earliest surviving long and substantial inscription in classical Sanskrit prose (Kavya style), marking the decisive transition from Prakrit to Sanskrit as the language of elite political panegyrics.
  • Reconstruction of Sudarshana Lake: Records that a catastrophic storm breached the embankment of the ancient Sudarshana reservoir. Rudradaman repaired and reinforced the dam from his own private royal purse (Koshata), explicitly emphasizing that he did so without imposing any extraordinary taxes (Pranaya) or forced unpaid labor (Vishti) upon his subjects!

Sudarshana Lake: Continuity Across Dynasties

Ruler / Period Specific Contribution to Sudarshana Lake

UPSC Dynasty Trap

Rudradaman I was a Shaka / Western Kshatrapa ruler of the Kardamaka dynasty, NOT a Kushana! UPSC frequently swaps rulers between the Shakas, Kushanas, and Satavahanas in matching questions.

Shaka–Parthian Administrative System: The Satrapal Structure

  • Shaka and Indo-Parthian rulers utilized satrapal administrative arrangements, reflecting wider Iranian / Achaemenid imperial traditions.
  • Territories were administered through provincial governors bearing Iranian-derived titles: Kshatrapa and Mahakshatrapa.
  • Subordinate rulers exercised considerable regional autonomy, frequently issuing their own coinage and royal inscriptions. This semi-autonomous structure is particularly important in understanding the long political longevity of the Western Kshatrapas.
Prelims Trap — Kshatrapa vs. Mahakshatrapa

Kshatrapa: Provincial governor or subordinate ruler within the satrapal administrative hierarchy.
Mahakshatrapa: Higher, superior imperial title ("Great Satrap").
Do NOT treat both as identical or interchangeable titles in UPSC objective options!

4. The Indo-Parthians (Pahlavas)

In the 1st century CE, following the Shakas and immediately preceding the rise of the Kushanas, political power in parts of the north-west passed to the Indo-Parthians (known in Indian texts as the Pahlavas), who originated from the Iranian plateau and established political authority in parts of north-western India.

  • Gondophernes (Gondophares): The most celebrated Indo-Parthian monarch (r. c. 19–46 CE), ruling from Taxila and Arachosia. His reign is confirmed by the Takht-i-Bahi Kharosthi inscription in Khyber Pakhtunkhwa.
  • St. Thomas Christian Tradition: An early Christian tradition recorded in the apocryphal Acts of Thomas claims that the apostle St. Thomas arrived from the Roman Mediterranean at the court of Gondophernes around 46 CE to propagate Christianity, before travelling south to the Malabar coast. UPSC notes must treat this as a strong ecclesiastical tradition rather than an independently established archaeological or historical fact.
  • Territorial Scope & Absorption: The Indo-Parthian realm was geographically confined to parts of modern eastern Iran, southern Afghanistan, and north-western Pakistan. The Parthians were gradually incorporated into the political and social landscape of the subcontinent before succumbing to the expanding Kushana confederacy.
Prelims Chronological Memory Chain

Indo-Greeks (2nd c. BCE) → Shakas (1st c. BCE) → Indo-Parthians (mid-1st c. CE) → Kushanas (1st–3rd c. CE)

5. The Kushanas: The Trans-Eurasian Colossus

Origins: The Yuezhi Migration & Xiongnu Pressure

The Kushanas were one of the five major constituent branches (or principalities) of the Yuezhi, a nomadic pastoralist confederacy originally inhabiting areas around north-western China (Gansu and Dunhuang). Driven westward by the mounting military pressure of the Xiongnu (Huns) around 165 BCE, the Yuezhi displaced the Shakas and moved into Bactria south of the Oxus (Amu Darya), where the Kushana branch emerged as the dominant political force.

  • Trans-Regional Corridor: Kushana expansion subsequently connected the Oxus region (Central Asia) with the north Indian plains (Ganga valley), giving the empire commanding geopolitical control over major trans-continental overland trade routes.
UPSC Correction — Yuezhi vs. Tocharians

Do NOT treat "Yuezhi" and "Tocharians" as unquestionable synonyms! The exact ethnic, genetic, and linguistic relationship between the nomadic Yuezhi and the Tocharian-speaking sedentary oasis populations of the Tarim Basin is a matter of ongoing scholarly debate. For UPSC purposes, remember precisely: The Kushanas were one branch of the Yuezhi.

UPSC Trap — Great Wall of China & Yuezhi Migration

• The westward movement of the Yuezhi was triggered by catastrophic defeats and military pressure from the nomadic Xiongnu (Huns) on the frontiers of China.
• The Great Wall of China was NOT built by the Kushanas, nor was it constructed specifically against or by the Yuezhi! It represents a long, multi-century process of frontier fortification by successive Chinese feudal states and imperial dynasties (Warring States, Qin, and Han).

UPSC Date Trap — Kanishka's Accession

Older textbooks traditionally equate Kanishka's accession with 78 CE (founding of the Shaka Era). However, modern numismatic and epigraphic discoveries (notably the Rabatak Inscription discovered in 1993 in Baghlan, Afghanistan) place Kanishka’s accession around 127 CE. UPSC questions recognize that the chronology of Kanishka remains historically debated; do not treat 78 CE as an unquestioned empirical fact.

3rd Century Decline — Sasanian Inscriptions & Kushanshahr

• By the 3rd century CE, the rise of the Sasanian Empire of Iran transformed the geopolitical balance. Sasanian monarch Shapur I (r. 240–270 CE) campaigned eastward, and his celebrated trilingual inscription at Ka'ba-ye Zartosht explicitly claims suzerainty over Hindustan (India) and Kushanshahr (the realm of the Kushanas up to Peshawar).
• Correction Note: Do not memorise that the Sasanians were the "first to use the term Hindustan"; earlier Achaemenid inscriptions (Darius I) had already used the Old Persian term Hi(n)dush. The vital UPSC takeaway is that Sasanian royal records explicitly document their annexation of Kushanshahr, reducing western Kushana territories to subordinate Kushano-Sasanian governors (Kushanshahs).

Kushana Administration & Royal Conception

  • Dual Political Capitals: Purushapura (Peshawar) and Mathura functioned as the two paramount political, military, and commercial centers of Kushana power in the Indian sphere.
  • Direct & Subordinate Rule: The empire contained areas under relatively direct royal supervision alongside territories administered by subordinate rulers bearing titles such as Kshatrapa and Mahakshatrapa.
  • Exalted Imperial Titulature:
    • Devaputra: "Son of God" or "Son of Heaven", reflecting the Kushana conception of the divine status of kingship, influenced by Chinese imperial practice (Tianzi).
    • "King of Kings" (Rajadhiraja / Maharaja / Shahanshah): Reflected imperial Iranian and Achaemenid royal traditions signifying suzerainty over a multitude of subordinate kings.
    • Kaisara / Caesar: Reflected imperial Roman prestige.
  • Dynastic Shrines (Devakulas): Constructed ancestral dynastic temples where royal statues were worshipped after death (e.g., the Mat shrine near Mathura, which yielded the famous headless statue of Kanishka).
High-Yield Prelims Pair — Kushana Royal Conception

Kushanas → Devaputra ("Son of Heaven / Divine Son") → Divine association of kingship
Kushanas → "King of Kings" (Rajadhiraja / Shahanshah) → Imperial suzerainty titulature

UPSC Administrative Correction — Meaning of "Shahanshah"

Do NOT interpret Shahanshahi as a term meaning "father and son ruling together"! Shahanshah ("King of Kings") is an Iranian imperial title signifying supreme suzerainty over subordinate or feudatory rulers; the practice of father-son co-rulership, while occasionally practiced, should never be memorised as the definition of the title.

Kushana Coinage: Cosmopolitan Religious Pluralism

  • Issued immense quantities of high-purity gold dinaras (weighing approx. 8 grams, patterned after the Roman denarius aureus) and heavy copper coins.
  • Pantheon of Multi-Religious Imagery: Kushana coins are a masterclass in religious syncretism, depicting deities from three distinct cultural realms:
    • Indian / Brahmanical & Buddhist: Shiva (Oesho) with trident and bull, Karttikeya (Skanda-Kumara, Vishakha), and the Buddha (inscribed BODDO in Greek script on Kanishka’s coins).
    • Iranian / Zoroastrian: Miiro (Mithra / Sun), Mao (Moon), Ardoxsho (Goddess of Fortune/Prosperity), Athsho (Sacred Fire), and Nana (Mother Goddess).
    • Hellenistic / Greco-Roman: Helios (Sun god), Selene (Moon goddess), and Herakles.

6. The Silk Route & Economic Transformation

The geopolitical nucleus of the Kushana Empire occupied the strategically indispensable crossroads of Eurasia, controlling the major overland transit corridors connecting China, Central Asia, India, and the Roman Empire.

Silk Route Tolls & Middlemen

The Kushanas controlled three key branches of the trans-Asian Silk Route passing through Xinjiang, Bactria, and Gandhara. By guaranteeing safe passage to caravan merchants (Sarthavahas), the Kushanas levied lucrative transit tolls and customs duties, enriching the royal treasury.

Roman Balance of Trade

Massive Roman demand for Indian luxury goods—spices (black pepper or Yavanapriya), fine muslins, silk, pearls, ivory, and gemstones—resulted in a colossal balance-of-trade surplus for India. Roman historian Pliny famously lamented the vast annual drain of Roman gold bullion into India.

Urban Growth & Guilds (Shrenis)

Commercial profits catalyzed a flourishing phase of urban growth across Taxila, Peshawar, Mathura, Kaushambi, Ujjain, and coastal ports (Bharuch/Bhrigukachchha). Autonomous craft guilds (Shrenis) acted as commercial banks, receiving permanent endowments from royal donors and merchants.

High Monetisation

While gold coinage facilitated wholesale long-distance maritime and Silk Route transactions, an immense volume of standardized copper coinage facilitated daily bazaar transactions down to the rural level.

UPSC Prelims Classic (1995 PYQ) — Yavanapriya = Pepper

• Literal Meaning: The ancient Sanskrit term Yavanapriya literally translates to "dear to the Yavanas (Greeks/Romans)".
• Commodity Identification: In ancient Indo-Roman commercial usage, it designated black pepper from the Malabar coast, which commanded sensational prices and astronomical demand in Mediterranean markets.
• UPSC PYQ Link: Directly tested in UPSC Prelims 1995 (Question: What does the ancient Indian term Yavanapriya denote? Answer: Pepper).

GS-I Mains Multi-Causal Formula

In UPSC Mains, never explain the economic boom of the Kushana era by the Silk Route alone! Structure your answer through the structural synergy: Centralized satrapal security + overland Silk Route junctions + Roman maritime trade + high-purity gold and copper coinage + organized merchant guilds (Shrenis) + expanding urban markets.

7. Social Impact & Cultural Assimilation

  • Progressive Social Incorporation: Foreign ruling groups (Indo-Greeks, Shakas, Pahlavas, Kushanas) were progressively integrated into Indian political and social structures rather than remaining alienated ruling enclaves. They adopted Indian names, embraced local religions, patronized Sanskrit and Prakrit, and entered into matrimonial alliances with indigenous royal dynasties (e.g., Shaka-Satavahana marriages).
  • Theoretical Varna Accommodation: Orthodox Brahmanical legalists found a theoretical mechanism to absorb these martial ruling groups into the four-varna hierarchy. The Manusmriti classified the Shakas, Yavanas, and Pahlavas as Vratya Kshatriyas ("degenerate / fallen Kshatriyas") who had lapsed from sacred rites due to the non-performance of Vedic rituals and absence of Brahmanical guidance.
  • Assimilation & Sanskritisation: This demonstrates an active historical process of social assimilation and Sanskritisation rather than permanent segregation of foreign groups from indigenous Indian society.
  • Craft Proliferation & Jatis: The massive expansion of commerce catalyzed extreme artisan specialization, giving rise to numerous sub-castes (Jatis) organized around hereditary craft guilds (weavers, potters, blacksmiths, ivory carvers).
UPSC Trap — Forms of Social Incorporation

Do not generalise that every foreign ruler was formally and universally accepted as an orthodox Kshatriya. The evidence reflects different forms and degrees of social and political incorporation, ranging from textual Brahmanical classifications (Vratya Kshatriyas) to pragmatic local matrimonial alliances.

8. Military Innovations & Material Culture

Central Asian contacts revolutionized military tactics, vocabulary, and everyday material culture across northern India:

Cavalry Warfare Revolution

Central Asian contacts established heavy cavalry and mounted archery as the decisive arm of battle, shifting away from heavy reliance on infantry and war elephants. The widespread use of reins, bits, and firm leather saddles facilitated far more effective mounted maneuvers.

Military Attire & Protective Gear

Introduced practical steppe equestrian clothing into India: stitched tailored trousers (pyjamas), long belted tunics/coats (sherwani prototypes), high leather riding boots (khapusa), helmets/caps, and protective scale armor—vividly depicted on Kushana royal coins and statuary.

Burnt-Brick Masonry & Tiles

Witnessed widespread archaeological evidence of high-fired burnt bricks for monumental construction, along with curved terracotta tiles for roofing and flooring across urban settlements from Taxila to Kausambi.

Pottery: Distinctive Red Ware

Distinctive Red Ware, including both plain and fine polished varieties (Red Polished Ware), emerged as a dominant ceramic marker in archaeological strata of this period.

  • Central Asian Military Vocabulary: Terms and technical practices relating to the horse, reins, saddles, and mounted combat became increasingly central to the military culture of north-western India. This should be studied as part of a broad, continuous process of trans-regional military interaction rather than as an isolated invention of a single dynasty.
Historical Accuracy — The Stirrup Question

Do NOT memorise as an uncontested fact that "The Kushanas introduced the stirrup into India." The chronology and exact evolution of the stirrup (early loose toe-loops vs. rigid iron frame stirrups) are historically debated among scholars; the evidence does not justify treating Kushana introduction of the stirrup as an unquestioned empirical fact.

Central Asian Urban Hub — Toprak-Kala (Khorezm)

• Toprak-Kala: A major fortified urban and palace-temple complex in ancient Khorezm (Uzbekistan, south of the Aral Sea), flourishing during the early centuries CE within the wider Central Asian cultural and commercial sphere.
• Numismatic Evidence: Kushana coins, including those of Vima Kadphises and Kanishka, have been unearthed at the site, providing concrete evidence of contemporary commercial and diplomatic interaction.
• Correction Note: Do NOT describe Toprak-Kala as a "Kushana imperial palace"! It was an indigenous Khorezmian royal and urban centre showing strong Kushana influence and trade contacts, not a direct capital of the Kushana empire.

9. Religious Transformations: Buddhism, Shaivism & Vaishnavism

Pluralistic Religious Interaction

  • The post-Mauryan and Kushana epoch witnessed intense interaction among Buddhism, Shaivism, Vaishnavism, and Iranian/Hellenistic religious traditions.
  • The Heliodorus Pillar at Besnagar (Vidisha):
    • Around 113 BCE, Heliodorus, a Greek ambassador sent by the Indo-Greek King Antialcidas to the court of the Shunga King Bhagabhadra, erected a famous monolithic stone Garuda pillar at Besnagar (ancient Vidisha, Madhya Pradesh).
    • The Brahmi inscription records that Heliodorus dedicated the pillar to Devadeva Vasudeva (Vishnu/Krishna) and styled himself a devout Bhagavata.
    • UPSC Significance: It is the earliest surviving epigraphic and archaeological proof of the adoption of Vaishnavism / Bhagavata devotion by a foreign Greek individual.
  • Cosmopolitan Coinage Pantheon: Kushana royal coinage vividly reflects this multi-religious coexistence, depicting Indian deities (Shiva/Oesho, Skanda, Buddha), Iranian deities (Mithra, Nana, Atsho), and Hellenistic deities (Helios, Selene, Herakles).

Institutionalisation & Spread of Mahayana Buddhism

  • Under Kushana patronage—particularly during the reign of Kanishka I—Buddhism underwent a decisive doctrinal and popular transformation, witnessing the institutional consolidation of Mahayana ("The Great Vehicle") Buddhism.
  • Deification of the Buddha: The Buddha was elevated from a revered human ethical philosopher into an eternal, transcendent deity endowed with cosmic grace, worshipped through devotional rituals (Bhakti) and anthropomorphic stone idols.
  • The Bodhisattva Ideal: Focus shifted toward compassionate beings (Bodhisattvas like Avalokiteshvara, Maitreya, and Manjushri) who delayed their own final Nirvana out of immense compassion to save all suffering sentient beings.
  • Pan-Asian Trans-Continental Transmission: Monks and merchant caravans travelling along Kushana-controlled Silk Route passes carried Buddhist scriptures and icons across the Pamirs into the Tarim Basin (Khotan, Kashgar, Kucha), Dunhuang, and mainland China, making Buddhism a world religion.
UPSC Doctrinal & Terminology Trap — Mahayana vs. "Hinayana"

• Mahayana: Literally means the "Great Vehicle".
• "Hinayana": Literally means the "Lesser Vehicle". However, modern historical scholarship recognizes that "Hinayana" was a polemical and sectarian label coined in Mahayana texts rather than a neutral or self-adopted title of an identifiable historical sect.
• Theravada ≠ Hinayana: Theravada should not simply be equated with Hinayana! Theravada represents a specific surviving orthodox Buddhist tradition based on the Pali canon, whereas "Hinayana" is a broad polemical category.
• Core Nuance: Mahayana was not merely a superficial "reform"; it represented profound doctrinal, devotional, and institutional shifts centered on the Bodhisattva ideal, universal compassion (Karuna), and skillful means (Upaya).

Evolution of Buddha Image Worship: From Aniconic to Anthropomorphic

  • The Aniconic Phase: In early Buddhist art (such as early Bharhut, Sanchi Stupa 1, and Bodh Gaya), the Buddha was never represented in human form. His presence was depicted strictly through aniconic symbols:
    • The Bodhi Tree: Representing Enlightenment (Bodhi).
    • The Empty Throne / Stool: Symbolizing supreme spiritual presence and meditation.
    • The Footprints (Buddhapada): Symbolizing his earthly wandering and teachings.
    • The Wheel (Dharmachakra): Representing the First Sermon at Sarnath (Turning of the Wheel of Law).
    • The Stupa: Representing the Buddha's Mahaparinirvana.
  • The Anthropomorphic Revolution: From around the 1st–2nd centuries CE, anthropomorphic (human-form) images of the Buddha emerged prominently and almost simultaneously in two distinct regions: Gandhara and Mathura.
Historical Accuracy — Gradual Evolution, Not Sudden Invention

Do NOT state in UPSC answers that "Buddhist image worship started suddenly out of nowhere under Kanishka." The impulse toward anthropomorphic representation developed gradually from the 1st century BCE, fueled by the popular demand for devotional worship (Bhakti), Yaksha-sculpting traditions, and doctrinal debates among Buddhist monastic communities. Kanishka's era provided the imperial consolidation, funding, and pan-Asian dissemination of these images.

The Fourth Buddhist Council at Kundalavana (Kashmir)

  • Venue & Patronage: Convened c. 72 / 100 CE at Kundalavana in Kashmir (or Jalandhar according to some traditions) under the patronage of Emperor Kanishka.
  • Leadership: Presided over by Vasumitra, with the celebrated poet and philosopher Ashvaghosha serving as vice-president.
  • Purpose & Outcome: Addressed doctrinal schisms among rival schools; codified the doctrines of the Sarvastivada sect.
  • The Mahavibhasha Shastra: The council compiled exhaustive encyclopedic Sanskrit commentaries on the Buddhist canon, known as the Mahavibhasha (engraved on copper sheets and encased in stone stupas).
UPSC Conceptual Nuance

Do NOT state that "Kanishka invented Mahayana Buddhism." Mahayana doctrinal concepts were already germinating earlier within Mahasanghika communities. Kanishka's imperial patronage provided the critical institutional, financial, and artistic catalyst for its consolidation, codification, and trans-continental expansion.

10. The Gandhara School of Art (Greco-Buddhist Art)

Gandhara Art Formula

Gandhara School = Indian Buddhist Spiritual Subject Matter + Hellenistic / Greco-Roman Classical Naturalism + Central Asian Sartorial Features.

Core Stylistic & Material Features

  • Geographical Zone: Flourihed in the ancient region of Gandhara (Peshawar valley, Rawalpindi, Taxila, Swat, and Jalalabad in eastern Afghanistan).
  • Medium / Material: Primarily carved in grey / blue-grey schist stone during the early phase (1st–2nd century CE). In the later phase (3rd–5th century CE), stucco (lime plaster), clay, and terracotta became predominant (e.g., Hadda, Taxila).
  • Greco-Roman Classical Influences:
    • Realistic, anatomically precise musculature and bone structure.
    • Wavy, flowing Greek-style hair gathered in a topknot (Ushnisha resembling Apollo).
    • Heavy, deep, realistic folds in monastic drapery (resembling the Roman toga) covering both shoulders.
    • Half-closed eyes indicating meditative serenity.
    • Architectural elements such as Corinthian capitals, acanthus leaf friezes, and cherub motifs.
  • Central Asian Features: Accompanying donor figures, kings, and Bodhisattvas are frequently depicted wearing Central Asian equestrian clothing: heavy boots, trousers, long tunics, and holding daggers.
Classic UPSC Mains & Prelims Trap

Gandhara art is NOT "purely Greek art"! While the outer aesthetic form, technique, and naturalism are Greco-Roman, the inner spiritual soul, iconographic symbolism, and thematic content are 100% Indian Buddhist (mudras, ushnisha, urna, lotus throne, Bodhisattva ideals).

11. Art, Architecture & Indigenous Traditions: Mathura, Sanchi & Pan-Indian Complexes

1. Yaksha–Yakshini Worship & Indigenous Roots

  • Ancient Nature & Fertility Cults: Yakshas and Yakshinis were popular local divinities associated with fertility, prosperity, vegetative nature, water, and village protection.
  • Independent Pre-Buddhist Existence: Their worship flourished independently across the subcontinent long before and alongside the rise of Buddhism and Jainism.
  • Assimilation into Monumental Art: Rather than being suppressed, Yaksha and Yakshini cults were absorbed directly into Buddhist artistic and religious traditions. Monumental carved representations appear prominently on the railings and gateways of Bharhut, Sanchi, and Mathura (e.g., the Didarganj Yakshi, Besnagar Yakshi, and Mathura tree-goddess Yakshis).
UPSC Prelims Analytical Link — Religious Syncretism

Yaksha–Yakshini traditions demonstrate that the expansion of Buddhism did not eliminate existing popular cults; rather, indigenous folk traditions and fertility deities were seamlessly incorporated into the Buddhist narrative and monumental architecture as guardians and attendants.

2. Core Features of the Mathura School of Art

  • Geographical Focus: Centered at Mathura on the Yamuna, the southern imperial capital of the Kushana Empire.
  • Material: Carved exclusively from indigenous white-spotted red sandstone quarried at Sikri.
  • Indigenous Aesthetic Continuity: Evolved directly out of the archaic Yaksha and Yakshi traditions of the Mauryan-Shunga era, characterized by full fleshy forms, broad muscular shoulders, expanding chests, and joyous spiritual vitality.
  • Pluralistic Religious Imagery: Unlike Gandhara (which was almost exclusively Buddhist), Mathura produced monumental icons for all three major contemporary religions:
    • Buddhism: Early anthropomorphic Buddha figures with shaved heads or snail-shell curls, energetic open eyes, smiling countenance, transparent drapery clinging closely to the body, seated on lion thrones under Bodhi trees with right hand in Abhaya Mudra.
    • Jainism: Hundreds of carved Ayagapatas (votive tablets) and seated/standing Tirthankara icons (excavated at Kankali Tila).
    • Brahmanical / Hindu: Earliest stone anthropomorphic representations of Shiva (as Linga and Mukhalinga), Vishnu (with four arms holding Shankha, Chakra, Gada, Padma), Surya in chariot, Balarama, and Durga Mahishasuramardini.
  • Secular Royal Statues: Famous headless, life-sized standing statue of Emperor Kanishka wearing a stiff nomadic coat, trousers, and heavy boots, holding a sword and mace, excavated at Mat near Mathura (royal ancestral shrine / Devakula).

4. Sanchi Stupa — Chronological Phasing & Structural Components

  • Original Ashokan Core: The original brick core of Stupa 1 (Great Stupa) at Sanchi was commissioned by Emperor Ashoka in the 3rd century BCE, built of unburnt brick.
  • Shunga Enlargement Phase: During the Shunga period (2nd century BCE), the stupa was enlarged to nearly double its original volume, encased in stone masonry, and provided with a high stone terrace (Medhi) and stone railings (Vedika).
  • Satavahana Torana Phase: The famous four elaborately carved gateways (Toranas) facing the four cardinal directions belong to a later phase, dated broadly to around the 1st century BCE, with inscriptions recording patronage from craftsmen under the Satavahanas (e.g., ivory carvers of Vidisha).
  • Narrative Jataka Sculptures: Elaborate narrative reliefs depicting episodes from the Jataka stories (previous births of the Buddha) and historic events appear across the Torana lintels and railing pillars.

Key Architectural Components of an Ancient Stupa

Stupa Component Architectural Meaning & Symbolic Function
Anda The solid hemispherical earthen/brick/stone mound forming the main dome of the stupa, symbolizing the cosmic egg and the mortal remains of the Buddha.
Harmika The square, balcony-like railing structure perched directly on the summit of the dome (anda), representing the abode of the gods and the sacred core.
Yasti The central vertical mast or pillar rising above the harmika, symbolizing the cosmic axis (axis mundi) connecting heaven and earth.
Chhatra / Chhatravali The umbrella (or tiered umbrellas) crowning the yasti, signifying royal honor, veneration, and sovereign spiritual protection.
Vedika The monumental stone railing or balustrade that encloses the sacred circumambulatory path (Pradakshina Patha) around the stupa mound.
Torana The ceremonial gateway oriented toward the cardinal directions, featuring two upright pillars and three arched crossbars richly covered with narrative sculpture.
Correction to Handwritten Notes — Multi-Phase Development of Sanchi

Do NOT write that all the Sanchi toranas and Jataka panels were simply "added by the Shungas"! The Great Stupa evolved through distinct historical strata:
• Mauryan Phase (Ashoka): Original brick mound core.
• Shunga Phase: Stone encasing, enlargement, lower balustrade (vedika), and terrace staircase.
• Later / Satavahana Phase: The four famous sculpted gateways (toranas) and narrative Jataka panels (c. 1st century BCE).

5. Ajanta Caves — Chronology & Patronage Clarification

  • Earliest Hinayana Phase: The earliest phase of cave excavations at Ajanta (Caves 9, 10, 12, 13, and 15A) belongs to the Satavahana period (c. 2nd century BCE to 1st century CE), representing early aniconic Buddhist monastic retreats.
  • Long Hiatus: Following the Satavahana era, excavation at Ajanta ceased for nearly three centuries.
  • Major Mahayana / Vakataka Phase: The magnificent later phase—including the world-renowned narrative murals, Chaitya hall façades, and elaborate Viharas—belongs to the Vakataka period (late 5th century CE, especially reign of Harishena).
UPSC Chronology Formulation — Ajanta Sequence

Ajanta should NOT be memorized simply as a "Shaka + Satavahana" monument! The authentic UPSC historical sequence is:
Early Satavahana phase (c. 2nd c. BCE – 1st c. CE) → Long multi-century hiatus → Major Vakataka phase (late 5th c. CE under Harishena).

6. Barabar Caves — Mauryan Rock-Cut Precedents

  • Oldest Rock-Cut Caves: The Barabar Caves (and adjacent Nagarjuni Caves in Bihar) represent the earliest surviving rock-cut architectural monuments in India.
  • Dynastic Patronage: Excavated during the Mauryan period under Emperor Ashoka (e.g., Sudama and Visvakarma caves) and his grandson Dasharatha (e.g., Gopika cave).
  • Sectarian Dedication: Dedicated primarily to the non-orthodox ascetic sect of the Ajivikas, founded by Makkhali Gosala.
  • Architectural Hallmark: Famous for their circular inner chambers and astonishing, glass-like interior Mauryan mirror polish on granite surfaces.
Contextual Distinction from Handwritten Notes

The Barabar caves are Mauryan (3rd century BCE), dedicated to Ajivikas, and are NOT a product of post-Mauryan Central Asian contacts! This distinction is highlighted here because Barabar caves frequently appear in student revision sheets alongside post-Mauryan rock-cut developments.

7. Pan-Indian Buddhist Art Landscape

Prelims Expansion — Buddhist Art Beyond Gandhara & Mathura

Do NOT restrict Buddhist art of this period exclusively to Gandhara and Mathura! The broader artistic landscape across the subcontinent during this epoch includes:
• Amaravati & Nagarjunakonda (Andhra / Deccan): Major Buddhist artistic centers flourishing in the Krishna-Guntur valley under the Satavahanas and Ikshvakus, celebrated for white limestone/marble narrative relief carvings.
• Western Indian Rock-Cut Caves: Monumental Buddhist Chaitya halls and Viharas carved across western India in Maharashtra (Karle, Bhaja, Bedsa, Kanheri, Nasik, Junnar).
• Central Indian Stupa Centres: Narrative stone carvings and railing additions at Bharhut, Sanchi, and Bodh Gaya.

12. Language, Literature, Theatre & Science

Evolution of Sanskrit & Elite Literature

  • Post-Mauryan inscriptions and literature display the active coexistence of Greek, Prakrit, and Sanskrit, with Sanskrit gradually gaining supreme prestige for elite political expression and courtly literature (exemplified by Rudradaman's Junagadh inscription).
  • Ashvaghosha: The celebrated court poet, dramatist, and spiritual counselor to Kanishka. He authored monumental works in chaste Sanskrit:
    • Buddhacharita: Complete Sanskrit poetic biography (Mahakavya) of Gautama Buddha, representing a landmark development in Sanskrit Buddhist literature.
    • Saundarananda: Sanskrit epic poem dealing with the conversion of Buddha’s handsome half-brother Nanda.
    • Shariputraprakarana: A 9-act drama discovered in Central Asia, recognized as the earliest surviving Sanskrit play in Indian literature.
  • Secular Sanskrit Literature: Vatsyayana’s Kamasutra belongs to the broader intellectual environment of the early centuries CE, offering an indispensable window into secular urban culture, citizen lifestyles (Nagaraka), and social customs.
  • Other Intellectual Luminaries: The Kushana intellectual circle is traditionally associated with Vasumitra (philosopher of the Fourth Council) and Nagarjuna (pioneer of the Madhyamaka philosophy).

The Indian Theatre & The "Yavanika" Debate

  • Later Indian classical theatrical terminology includes the word Yavanika, designating the stage curtain.
  • Traditional accounts associate this with the influence of Yavanas (Greeks) on theatrical curtains and staging conventions.
Accuracy Note — The Yavanika Tradition

The exact extent of direct Greek influence on the development of Indian drama and the stage curtain term Yavanika is debated among scholars. For UPSC, treat this association as a recognized point of traditional cultural contact rather than an absolutely established causal historical fact.

Scientific Synthesis: Astronomy & Astrology

  • Greek and Hellenistic contacts contributed significantly to the mutual exchange of ideas in astronomy and astrology.
  • Indian astronomical and astrological traditions absorbed and adapted elements of Yavana / Hellenistic knowledge, integrating the seven-day week system and planetary coordinates.
  • The Brihat Samhita of Varahamihira famously noted:
    "The Greeks are foreigners, yet the science of astronomy is well established among them. Therefore, they are honored even like sacred rishis."
  • Indian astronomers adapted five early astronomical treatises (Siddhantas), including the Romaka Siddhanta (Roman influence) and Paulisa Siddhanta (Alexandrian influence of Paulus).
UPSC Correction — Etymology of "Horoscope" & "Horashastra"

Do NOT write that the English word "horoscope" was derived from the Sanskrit word "horashastra"! The direction of influence is the exact reverse: Greek horoskopos ("hour-watcher") is the source of the Western term, while ancient Indian astrological traditions later incorporated Hellenistic knowledge and developed technical vocabulary around terms such as horā.

Metallurgy & Struck Coinage Technology

  • The development of more precisely made struck coins (using two matched steel/bronze dies) represented a dramatic technological and monetary advance over earlier indigenous punch-marked traditions, enabling standardized weights, high-relief portraiture, and dual-script inscriptions.

The Charaka Medical Tradition

  • The medical literature associated with the Charaka tradition (the foundational compendium Charaka Samhita) records extensive scientific knowledge of medicinal plants, herbs, pathology, anatomy, and therapeutic preparations.
Charaka Court Association Caution

The exact historical association between a specific personal physician named Charaka and the court of Kanishka is uncertain. For UPSC examinations, focus safely on the historical importance of the Charaka medical tradition rather than asserting an undisputed personal court relationship.

13. Architecture & Material Culture

  • Monumental Brick Architecture: The Kushana epoch witnessed massive expansion in the use of high-fired burnt bricks for fortifications, stupas, monasteries, and domestic urban complexes.
  • Terracotta Tiles: Advanced roofing and flooring technology utilizing interlocking terracotta tiles became common across urban settlements from Taxila to Kausambi.
  • Kanishka's Great Stupa at Peshawar (Shah-ji-ki-Dheri): Chinese pilgrims (Fa-Hien, Xuanzang) described Kanishka’s wooden and brick pagoda-stupa at Purushapura as one of the wonders of the ancient Asian world, rising over 600 feet across thirteen storeys. Excavations yielded the famous gilded bronze Kanishka Reliquary inscribed with Kanishka's name.

20. UPSC Traps Matrix — Must-Revise Distinctions

Common Examination Trap Accurate Historical & Conceptual Reality
Indo-Greeks were the first Greeks to enter India Alexander the Great entered the north-west in 326 BCE; Indo-Greeks established post-Alexandrian Hellenistic political rule in parts of north-western India in the 2nd century BCE.
Milinda-panha authored by Nagarjuna Nagasena was the Buddhist sage in the dialogue; Nagarjuna was the later founder of Madhyamaka philosophy (tested in Prelims 2023).
Rudradaman I was a Kushana emperor Rudradaman I belonged to the Kardamaka dynasty of Western Kshatrapas (Shakas).
Vikramaditya in 57 BCE is a verified historical fact The story of Vikramaditya defeating Shakas in 57 BCE is traditional / legendary and not securely established by contemporary epigraphy or archaeology.
Kshatrapa and Mahakshatrapa were identical ranks Kshatrapa was a provincial governor or subordinate ruler; Mahakshatrapa was a superior imperial title ("Great Satrap").
"Shahanshah" means father and son ruling together Shahanshah is an Iranian imperial title meaning "King of Kings"; father-son co-rulership, while practiced, is NOT the meaning of the title.
The Kushanas definitely introduced the stirrup to India The chronology and introduction of the stirrup are historically debated; it must not be memorised as an uncontested empirical fact.
"Horoscope" was derived from Sanskrit "Horashastra" The direction of transmission is the reverse: Greek horoskopos is the source of the Western term; Indian astrology later adapted Hellenistic concepts around horā.
Charaka was definitely the personal court physician of Kanishka The personal court link is historically uncertain; candidates should focus on the significance of the Charaka medical tradition.
Gandhara Art was purely Greek / foreign It was a syncretic Buddhist art tradition: Greek/Roman in external aesthetic form, but 100% Indian Buddhist in religious soul.
Buddhist art in this era was confined to Gandhara and Mathura Buddhist art flourished across the subcontinent, including Amaravati and Nagarjunakonda (Andhra) and western cave complexes (Karle, Bhaja, Kanheri).
Kanishka invented Mahayana Buddhism Kanishka was a crucial imperial patron who consolidated, patronized, and facilitated the spread of Mahayana; he did not invent it.
All Central Asian rulers were ethnically identical They came from distinct cultural backgrounds: Indo-Greeks (Bactrian Greeks), Shakas (Scythians), Indo-Parthians (Iranians), and Kushanas (Yuezhi).
Kanishka's accession was definitely in 78 CE The chronology is intensely debated; while 78 CE is the traditional Shaka Era date, modern discoveries (Rabatak inscription) favor c. 127 CE.
Yuezhi and Tocharians are exact synonyms Scholarly debate surrounds their exact ethnolinguistic identity; for UPSC, remember that Kushanas were one branch of the Yuezhi; do NOT treat Yuezhi and Tocharians as identical synonyms.
Great Wall of China was built by or for the Kushanas The Great Wall represents centuries of frontier fortifications by successive Chinese dynasties against Xiongnu nomadic pressure; it was neither built by nor for the Kushanas.
All Sanchi Toranas and Jataka panels were added by the Shungas Sanchi evolved in distinct phases: Ashokan brick core → Shunga stone encasement & stone vedika → four elaborate Toranas and Jataka reliefs added in 1st c. BCE under Satavahana patronage.
Rudradaman's record is simply "the first Sanskrit inscription" Safer UPSC formulation: It is the earliest surviving long and substantial inscription in classical Sanskrit prose (150 CE).
Sasanians mentioned "Hindustan" for the very first time Do not treat "first time" as an unquestionable claim; the key UPSC takeaway is that Shapur I's 3rd-century CE inscription explicitly mentions Hindustan and Kushanshahr, confirming Kushana decline.
Toprak-Kala was a Kushana royal palace It was a major Khorezmian fortified palace-city; Kushana coin finds show extensive cross-border trade and interaction, not that it was a Kushana dynastic palace.
Ajanta is a "Shaka + Satavahana" monument Ajanta's authentic sequence: Early Satavahana phase (Caves 9 & 10) → multi-century hiatus → Major Vakataka phase (late 5th c. CE murals under Harishena).
Barabar Caves belong to Central Asian contact era Barabar Caves are Mauryan (3rd c. BCE), excavated by Ashoka and Dasharatha for the Ajivikas with mirror polish (NOT post-Mauryan Central Asian).
Yavanapriya refers to Greek women or court entertainers Literally "dear to the Yavanas", it was the Sanskrit commercial term for Indian black pepper (direct UPSC Prelims 1995 PYQ).
"Hinayana" and Theravada are interchangeable synonyms Theravada is an authentic surviving early Buddhist tradition; "Hinayana" ("Lesser Vehicle") was a later polemical label used in Mahayana texts, not an objective self-descriptor.
Buddha image worship was invented overnight under Kanishka Image worship was a gradual evolution from earlier aniconic symbols (wheel, footprints, empty throne) to anthropomorphic statues developed concurrently in Gandhara and Mathura in 1st–2nd c. CE.
